The day we returned from our Viking river cruise to southern France (a series of missives will be forthcoming), I received a message from KaLeigh - the CEO of *Westwin Elements* - to see if I could attend a meeting in Oklahoma City. Ima washed up the clothes and we hopped in the Buick for the 880-mile drive to OKC.

Along the way, we passed the beautiful *Hard Rock Casino & Resort Tulsa* just north of the city. We have passed the complex several times over the years, and so we decided to stop by to get a burger and fries and, of course, a t-shirt. Life's experiences are best traced through the trail of t-shirts. In this vein, over the years, I have accumulated more than my share of Hard Rock Cafe shirts: Singapore, Amsterdam, Vienna, Bangkok, Jakarta, Kuala Lumpur, Sydney, Gatlinburg and even a knock-off version from Manila. Unfortunately, most of them are in a storage tub somewhere.

At any rate, Ima and I walked into the casino, close to the entrance where a giant electric guitar stands, found a diner that was open and located the gift shop. In the diner, a young lady named Brandy served our table (I asked if she was named after the song) and we enjoyed a good ol' burger and fries and chocolate milkshake. Ima had their onion rings and a salad. The diner wasn't all that busy, so I asked Brandy about the history of the casino and resort. She told me the casino complex formerly belonged to the Cherokee tribe and had sold it to the corporation which owned Hard Rock.

Her reply chased me to my *Gemini* chatbot for two reasons: I am always interested in the background of Native American casinos, especially in Oklahoma; and secondly, a story from our family history. So, as Ima settled behind the wheel - and we headed west on I-44 with its 80-mph speed limit toward Oklahoma City - I consulted the algorithms for the story behind the casino.

I learned that the Cherokee Nation still owned and operated the complex. It was a popular misconception that it had been sold. The tribe had entered into a branding agreement to use the Hard Rock name. The site actually had its origins as a bingo hall in Catoosa, Oklahoma in the 1990s. Then it was called the Cherokee Bingo Outpost. In 2004 - following the expansion of tribal gaming laws in Oklahoma - the Cherokee Nation opened a \$80 million, 150,000-square-foot resort at the location with a nearby golf course. (Any of you who have driven I-44, a toll road through various tribal territories, know there is a string of Native American casinos that dot the highway.)

Five years later (2009), the Cherokee Nations Entertainment division sought to elevate the resort's profile and entered into a licensing agreement with Hard Rock International to rebrand the site. What I found really interesting in all this is that the Hard Rock enterprise is now owned by the Seminole Tribe of Florida!

The Hard Rock of Tulsa is now the "crown jewel" of the Cherokee Nation's gaming operations. The resort employs over 4,000 people, attracted more than 10 million visitors last year, and paid over \$22 million in "exclusivity fees" to the State of Oklahoma, most of which goes to the state's public education fund. Last year alone, for example, the resort generated over \$1 billion in revenue (including gaming, hotel stays, dining, and "The Joint" entertainment venue which hosts a number of high-profile entertainers as advertised by giant, colorful billboard displays that can be seen from the interstate. Among the performers: Bob Dylan, Stevie Wonder, Billy Idol, Toto, Cheap Trick, Clint Black, Tracy Lawrence, and Larry the Cable Guy). Over the past decade, the tribal gaming operations - led by the Hard Rock site - have provided over \$1.2 billion in dividends directly to the Cherokee Nation government to fund public services.

How is the money spent? Unlike corporate-owned casinos such as those in Las Vegas, where profits go to shareholders, 100% of the net income from the Tulsa casino stays within the tribe and the local community and is split as follows: 65% goes to "tribal services" (a general fund that pays for tribal healthcare - the Cherokee tribe operates the largest tribally-run health system in the U.S. - education, housing, and language preservation). 35% goes to "reinvestment" (the portion kept by Cherokee Nation Businesses to maintain the property, fund expansions, and diversify into other industries).

As I was thinking about the Cherokee Nation's casino properties, I remembered one of my favorite series on *A&E* and then *Netflix* that concerned the dynamics of a small-town Wyoming sheriff dealing with a nearby Native American casino. The series was called *Longmire* and was - simply put - the best thing I had seen on television in a long time (the series ended in 2017). The show, named after the fictional main character Walt Longmire was set in fictional Absaroka County, Wyoming, and as part of the tension in the show, involved jurisdictional clashes between Longmire and individuals associated with a nearby Cheyenne Reservation casino ("The Four Winds Casino").

It was a great series. I heartily recommend it for a night or two, or three, of binge watching. In my view, each episode is incredibly well-written and draws on a rich cast of characters and realistic social tensions. The series is based on the characters in over twenty books by Craig Johnson (starting with the *The Cold Dish* in 2004). Johnson, who served as the executive consultant on the series, is the real deal: he still resides in a small Wyoming town, along with 25 other citizens, and built his own ranch in the 1980s; he always looks like he just stepped off the ranch wearing his signature denim jacket and well-worn cowboy hat; he remains deeply involved in the annual "Longmire Days" festival in Buffalo, Wyoming (the real-life inspiration for the fictional Durant); and reportedly fought with the show's producers who wanted to make Walt younger.

I am now reading *The Cold Dish* and Johnson's description of the character Lucian Connally - the one-legged, foul-mouthed retired sheriff who preceded Walt - may be the very best I have ever read.<Footnote number={1} /> Almost every one of Johnson's books are based on real-life newspaper articles (he constantly scans articles appearing in local papers in Wyoming and Montana for unusual crimes or social issues around which he can build plot lines). Moreover, Johnson has been widely praised for his treatment of native American culture and works closely with members of the Northern Cheyenne tribe to ensure that his portrayal of the reservation and its people is respectful and accurate, rather than stereotypical. As a result, last year Johnson was recognized with four lifetime achievement awards - including the Western Writers of America - and his books have been translated into 14 languages.

What about the story from our family history? I know some of you have heard this story before. My fraternal grandfather (Wiley F. Akers) and his brothers - all of whom were orphans - had enough Cherokee blood that they qualified for the last Oklahoma land rush. Seems my great-great grandmother was a full-blooded Cherokee princess. At any rate, Grandpa Wiley and his brothers scrounged up all their personal funds and entrusted the youngest of the brothers to participate in the land rush and buy land. On the train ride to Oklahoma the youngest brother got flimflammed in a card game and lost all the family money.

Did I tell you how much the Akers love their card games?

It is a familiar family story. I like to think the land that could have been purchased later became valuable oil land or perhaps the very property on which the *Hard Rock Casino & Resort Tulsa* now stands.

Please don't pop my notional bubble of near wealth.

When Ima and I arrived in OKC we stayed with KaLeigh at her new house and small farm, along with her husband Jason and her two children Mary and John. As I joined KaLeigh for meetings at the office, Ima spent time with KaLeigh's mom, Martha. We had a delightful visit including a wonderful Good Friday service and KaLeigh even arranged for a ticket to see the Oklahoma Thunder NBA team play the Los Angeles Lakers.

On the final night of our stay, a loud siren blast warned us that tornados were about to touch down in the area. We hustled to the tornado shelter - a concrete and metal bunker buried in KaLeigh's garage - where we all huddled underground. KaLeigh tried to get updates on the storm on her laptop computer and reassure the kids that they were safe. It was Ima and my first experience in a tornado shelter and hopefully the last. But what an experience!

The next day, Ima and I hit the road again, passing the casino complex for the second time, and driving all the way back to Ohio.

By then, the first four books in Johnson's Longmire series were sitting on our doorstep.
