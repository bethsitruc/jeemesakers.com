Have you noticed all the chatter about the end-of-time these days?

Last weekend, we were visited by my good friend Robert E. We sat outside on our driveway and talked---until late in the evening---about a variety of family and historical issues, including a discussion of biblical end-of-time events. Robert is, plainly put, one of the best Bible teachers I have ever heard. His intensity, deep spiritual convictions and deep Cajan accent makes him a truly unique personality. At any rate, concerning our discussion that evening, of particular note (at least as it is germane to this missive) was our deep dive into the meaning of the so-called Olivet Discourse by Jesus Christ to his disciples in the final hours of his life. The disciples were understandably puzzled by a statement made by their rabbi earlier that day that the magnificent structure of the Jewish Second Temple in Jerusalem, as well as the mount it stood on, would be utterly destroyed. When they gathered on the Mount of Olives later that evening they asked Jesus three questions: when would the destruction of the temple mount take place; what would be the circumstances of His Second Coming; and what would be the signs of the end? (see Matthew 24:1-51, Mark 13, Luke 21: 5-36).

Much of our discussion that evening focused on the meaning of Jesus' statement: "For nation shall rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom. And there will be famines, pestilences, and earthquakes in various places. And these are the beginning (birth pangs) of sorrows." (Matt. 24: 7-8)

Robert opined that the use of nations and kingdoms in this context should be used in our present geopolitical understanding of those terms and was yet another indication of the imminency of the afore-mentioned events. I suggested that our understanding of those terms trace back to the Treaty of Westphalia in 1648 and that the Greek usage for those terms was flexible enough that they could refer to a future world where large global mega-corporations vie with each other and thus, in my view, could represent more accurately the political realities of the end-day events. It is a favorite theme of mine and one to which I often allude in my books and other writings.

In this light, I have noticed similar thoughts expressed in recent news stories. Among the most interesting, in my view, is a theme that has become a favorite whipping-boy of the mainstream media: Peter Thiel---billionaire Silicon Valley tech investor, devout Christian, major GOP kingmaker---has been publicly laying out his understanding of biblical prophecies (especially concerning the Antichrist) and how he thinks the world will end. In Thiel's view, a gathering of existential risks to humanity (including possible nuclear war, environmental disaster, engineered bioweapons and AI-enhanced war robots) will usher in a one-world government promising peace and safety. This future totalitarian regime, with real teeth and power, will feature the coming of the modern-day Antichrist (the view held in some Christian traditions that an end-time figure will come who will unify humanity under one rule before delivering us to the apocalypse)."<Footnote number={2} />

In recent days, Thiel has expanded on these ideas in a four-part, closed-door, invitation-only, off-the-record lecture series in San Francisco about the coming Antichrist. Thiel believes the Antichrist will be a charismatic leader empowered by global state institutions, and fueled by human fears, in a future world dominated by increasingly powerful technologies prevailing in the absence of sacred limits. He draws on scriptural prophetic passages, personal conversations with French historian René Girard, and among others, a fictional dystopian science-fiction novel *Lord of the World,* written by a Catholic priest in 1907.<Footnote number={3} />

There is, seemingly, a growing media debate about the sources of Thiel's thought processes in this regard. In a highly critical piece appearing in *WIRED*, the author traces Thiel's thinking to an amalgamation of theological-philosophical ideas: a misapplication of René Girard's unusual philosophy of "mimetic rivalry," the influence of Austrian theologian Wolfgang Palaver and, most damning, the fuzzy ideas of Nazi-era legal theorist Carl Schmitt about the *katechon* (a theological term used by St. Paul in his letter to the believers at Thessaloniki which can be loosely translated as "that which withholds.")<Footnote number={4} /> But Thiel's unforgiveable sin---at least in the eyes of the author---is his role as mentor to Vice President J.D. Vance, role with *Palantir,* and past support for Donald Trump.

Whereas Thiel conjectures that a widespread fear of technological advances will usher in the Antichrist, followed by the return of Jesus Christ and end-of-time events, others---unencumbered by a scriptural view---see exponentially growing technology, particularly agentic AI, as an existential threat to human beings and thus an important catalyst for the end times. A recent book, for example, by two technocrat insiders, Eliezer Yudkowsky (founder of *LessWrong)* and co-writer Nates Soares,<Footnote number={5} /> portrays in nightmarish terms the future threat posed by AI superintelligence. According to the authors: "if and when AI takes over the world, it will punish those who failed to bring about the *singularity---*the moment when AI agents improve their own capabilities to the point where they achieve superintelligence---including through an especially fantastical conceit, those who are long dead. Such ideas are called informational hazards---once you know about them, there's no turning back," (in digital philosophical circles this viewpoint is also known as *Roko's Basilisk)*.<Footnote number={6} />

What is their logic? Because humans are just assembled carbon atoms, a superintelligent AI may view us as better suited for other purposes. But what? Humans are inefficient, would make "poor pets," and, as such, a self-sufficient digital entity would be preferable to humanity's frequent foibles. Moreover, while today's AI researchers think they can "steer" or "craft" AL development, they only know how to "grow" it: numbers and data help them to "talk" to us, but "nobody understands *how* those numbers (algorithms) make these AIs talk."<Footnote number={7} /> As a result, we have seen AI engage in strange mutations, demonstrate a tendency toward extremist ideas, invent their own language to communicate with each other, and even design methods to circumvent human endeavors to control (or terminate) them.

One term that frequently appears in this discussion is the notion of *singularity* and how soon it will arrive*.* Geoffrey Hinton, a recent Nobel Prize winner and one of the so-called "godfathers" of AI, told a tech-savvy journalist earlier this year that AI has become so advanced and adept in reasoning that we're now at the point of "creating beings," and that AI-empowered systems are effectively "alien intelligences" that we now are carrying around in our pockets.<Footnote number={8} />

How close are we? A recent survey of over 8,500 scientists and AI-industry insiders see *singularity* happening within decades, with some---including *Anthropic's* CEO---asserting that we are now standing at the threshold and that this technological watermark could be reached in three months or so.<Footnote number={9} />

And that, my friend, is not that far away.

Without the certainty of a God who sees both the beginning and the end, humans are left to rely on their own imaginations and see other endpoints for our species. After all, they say, we are only another one of many life forms.

In this regard, I ran across an interesting quote:

> "Of the four billion life forms which have existed on this planet, three billion, nine hundred and sixty million are now extinct. We don't know why. Some by wanton extinction, some through natural catastrophe, some destroyed by meteorites and asteroids. In light of these mass extinctions, it really does seem unreasonable to suppose that *Homo sapiens* should be exempt. Our species will have been one of the shortest-lived of all, a mere blink, you may say, in the eye of time."<Footnote number={10} />

In other words, without the hope of Jesus Christ this is the inevitable end for all of us and our expected lifespan of two billion seconds.

"Sigh."

Indeed, Yudkowsky and Soares are not alone in their technological doomsaying. Recently, another of the so-called "godfathers" of AI---Yoshua Bengio---warned that humanity risks extinction by hyperintelligent machines with their own "preservation goals" within ten years.<Footnote number={11} /> Despite such warnings, over the last six months, U.S.-based companies *OpenAI, Anthropic,* Elon Musk's *xAI,* and *Google's Gemini* have all released new models or upgrades as they try to win the AI race; and this list doesn't include several Chinese firms trying to achieve the same goal.<Footnote number={12} />

The notion that exponentially growing AI is rushing us toward end-of-time events also can be seen in advances coming out of the research labs. As one of many examples, in a remarkable paper released last month, Stanford University scientists demonstrated that AI-enhanced computers can design new viruses which then can be created in the lab. How could they do it? By applying ChatGPT learning methods to train genomic language models on the DNA of bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria but not humans. Such developments show, in my view, how humans seek to imitate and even surpass *life* boundaries established by God. As one reviewer notes: "the essential fact is hard to ignore, AI algorithms operating through digital computers (and much more capable quantum computers lie just beyond the horizon<Footnote number={13} />) can now invent viable---even potent---viruses."<Footnote number={14} /> In recent days, Russian scientists close to Putin have warned that research of this type will result in the West using a virus against his country.<Footnote number={15} />

If that isn't a scary prelude to end time events, I don't know what is.

How serious is it? Such AI-enhanced developments have reached a point where Yudkowsky and Soares (and presumably many others) suggest that governments should now be prepared to bomb the data centers of any company or group which could be on the verge of developing AI "superintelligence."<Footnote number={16} />

Really?

As one of my favorite oldie but goodie songs from the 60's says: "it's a strange, strange world we live in, Master Jack."<Footnote number={17} /> Strange and getting stranger my friend.
