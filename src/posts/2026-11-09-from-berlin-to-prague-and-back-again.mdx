Ima and I just returned from a 14-day trip of our lifetimes: a *Viking* river cruise from Berlin to Prague, with brief stops in Potsdam, Wittenberg, Torgau, Meissen, Saxony’s “Switzerland,” Dresden and Prague. Unfortunately, a historic shortage of rainfall meant record low water levels on the Elbe River. That meant our *Viking* boats (the *Beyla* and its sister ship the *Astrild),* remained docked at Wittenberg and Dresden respectfully, didn’t move an inch down the river and were more like floating hotels than true river boats. For most of our trip we instead were transported overland by motor coaches between historic sites. The *Viking* staff—and their incredible guides—did what they could to make the trip enjoyable and informative, but we did miss the scenery of floating down the historic river.

Our first stop was Berlin. We opted for a two-day extension in the capital city of a reunified Germany, largely based on the expectation that Dr. Fred Mullinax would be with us. Fred, the former president of Alice Lloyd College and vice-president of the College of the Ozarks—and a long-time friend—had spent time studying in Germany and was very knowledgeable of Berlin and the city’s history. Fred’s wife Rachel is also a joy to travel with (I previously traveled with the couple to the wartime Normandy beaches and a visit to American wartime cemeteries, and Ima and I traveled with them earlier this year on a river cruise in southern France from Nice on the Riviera to Avignon and Lyons). But shortly before we were scheduled to depart, Rachel’s doctors advised her against taking the trip for medical reasons. We missed them. In addition, my sister Debbie had to drop out—so what was planned originally as a traveling party of eight became four. Ima and I were joined by Carole and Kathy, also close friends of Fred and Rachel, and I think the only reason they didn’t drop out was because they didn’t want to abandon us.

We will always be grateful to them both.

The last time I had been in Berlin was over forty years ago, when I was researching (for my thesis) General Prince Avalov-Bermondt, a Cossack pro-monarchist military leader who played a significant role in the Russian civil war and then emigrated to Germany during the Nazi era. He formed a proto-Fascist movement of Russian political emigres living in Berlin during this era. I still plan to write a biography of the flamboyant, and in some ways tragic, military leader. He represents, in my mind, a long since forgotten world where the great European dynasties—the Hohenzollern, Romanov and Habsburg—controlled, in part, the destiny of the continent. All that was cut short by the tumultuous four-year bloodbath of World War I (1914-1918) which, among other things, resulted in the collapse of each of these dynasties.

Thus, while I was anxious to see how Berlin had changed since my previous visit, I was also there on an information gathering mission. In my upcoming historical fiction novel, *The Kaiser’s Brooch,* I included several scenes set in Wilhelmine Berlin during the mid-1890s. I hoped to gather the kind of ground-truth details that would add realistic flourishes to the text narrative.

Much to my chagrin, however, there was very little information (or, for that matter, a desire to recall or preserve such information) about the Wilhelmine era in Berlin and elsewhere or of the last German Emperor Kaiser Wilhelm II. No doubt part of the reason for this is the long list of tragic events which have plagued Germany since the end of World War I: the tumultuous Weimar period, the catastrophic Nazi era and World War II, and the division of the country for over four decades.

Here and there I encountered only snippets of the Wilhelmine world: a visit to the New Palace in Potsdam (now in the process of a major restoration), the Humboldt Forum (a major cultural and scientific center located inside the reconstructed façade of the former imperial palace in Berlin), and the church and museums on an island in the Spree River (*Museumsinsel)*. Gone was the *Rote Burg* on *Alexanderplatz* (the “red castle” that formerly served as the Royal Prussian Police Headquarters for the city), which serves as a location for a couple chapters in my book. Another setting for my book, the statue of Neptune, was surrounded by a construction barrier as it was being relocated to a platz near the old church of Mary. Finally, the old historic Hotel Adlon was destroyed but a replica has been erected near the Brandenburg Gate.

I’m sure that is more than you wanted to know about a world that is—for all intents and purposes—no longer. More to the point, that is the time frame in which I set my writings.

I’m not even sure I can explain why.

My sister had joined me for my previous trip to Berlin, at that time a divided city which served as a flashpoint at the height of the Cold War. For example, the Brandenburg Gate itself was situated in a no-man’s land between the walls that separated the two opposing parts of Berlin. (It would be there for 42 years in total). Seems hard to imagine now, but in those days, we had to enter West Berlin by one of the three railroad corridors across East Germany.

But that was *then.* In short, Ima and I loved our most recent trip to Berlin and hope to return there. We stayed in the vibrant *Potsdamer Platz* area and our hotel was within walking distance of the Brandenburg *Tor* (Gate), the sobering experience of the “Topography of Terror” (located on the site of the former Gestapo headquarters), and Checkpoint Charlie, one of the east-west crossing points during the Cold War and the site of an incident that nearly started World War III. Our hotel was also close to a shopping mall and several restaurants, including a great German restaurant and a nearby *Burgermeister* where we munched on a burger and fries every bit as good as *Five Guys.*

I’ll include more about the sites in Berlin—as well as our subsequent stops—in future missives. But I did want to make a general observation about the trip before I post (and write) these missives.

I always return from each trip abroad with, among other things, a spiritual realization or two. I never intend this to happen. This time was no different. On this trip I bumped into two such spiritual realizations; one is of the glass half-full variety, the other a half-empty glass. As you will see, I have a difficult time putting the latter into words.

First, the glass half-full observation. One of the great things about these cruises, and it usually happens during conversations at the dinner table, you get to learn more about friends. In this case, Ima and I learned more about Kathy and her family. (We hadn’t seen Kathy since the funeral of her remarkable husband Glen). In this case, our conversation moved to praying for friends. I mentioned that in making such requests, St. Paul tells us we must approach the heavenly throne with a spirit of thankfulness (Philippians 4:6). Carole sprang to life at that remark and went on to tell us about her personal “Gratitude Journal”—she never goes to sleep without thanking God for something positive in her life that day.

What a wonderful thought! In truth, that spiritual gem was more valuable to me than all the Reformation sites in Wittenberg combined. I determined on the spot to make that insight a part of my own spiritual DNA.

The second spiritual realization, the glass half-empty, is a bit harder to express. Please forgive me if the following comes across as disjointed. The bottom line: during our time in Europe, I observed and sensed what I have come to call a prevalent “cultural numbness” there. Moreover, I fear this malaise will eventually spread to our country (if not already here).

What do I mean?

Much of our time on this trip was spent in areas formerly controlled by Communist regimes. The inhabitants, those who stayed and were not expelled, spent decades under government and educational “control” and monitoring systems designed to suppress any notion of true Christian living, undermine the value of true individuality and critical thinking and, of course, disparage any notional value of capitalism.

In essence, people have been robbed of any sense of the importance of Jesus Christ as the Way, the Truth and the Life. Not that the Communist overlords destroyed that many physical churches—these structures remained intact—but these mostly were empty of parishioners; they were empty shells. The ruling elites focused instead on undermining the faith of the people.<Footnote number={2} />

My point? We are seeing many groups touting very similar ideas—under a Western modernist veneer and guise—in our own country today.

Our most recent midterm elections, in my view, only reinforced these tendencies.

The result? In East Germany, successive generations of citizens deprived of any sense of true purpose in life—superficial “empty suits” just going through the motions of life—became walking and talking fodder for manipulation. That’s the true meaning of control in any society, including our own. In short, these are the necessary ingredients of the worldwide spirit of despair and lawlessness that the Apostle Paul warns will characterize collective worldviews as we approach end-time events.

Even then, this is not as straight-forward as it seems. (And this was one of the real “eye-openers” for me on this trip). Indeed, from our time in Germany there is an important qualifier or two to point out. The euphoria that accompanied the fall of the Wall in Berlin, the collapse of East Germany, and the fascinating Velvet Revolution in Prague was, in many cases short-lived. The psychological and material adjustment to reunification in Germany, for example, was extremely difficult for many people on both sides of the border. I’ll never forget an observation made by our guide for the history of East Berlin, Lauren van Vuuren (a South African writer and teacher at *Freie Universität* in the city). During her outstanding tour of the former *Stasi*—East Germany’s Secret Police—headquarters, she made a comment that has stuck with me ever since: “freedom does not matter if you have no means to compete.”

There is an important truth contained therein that we in the United States ignore at our peril.

For some reason, I feel the following observation is appropriate to include at this juncture. As many of you know, at the same time we were traveling in Germany and the Czech Republic, (October 14-28, 2026) much of the international media—and certainly the European media—focused on the large gatherings of mostly young people in several of the larger urban cities of France, (especially Paris), Spain and Italy. These gatherings invariably turned violent.

Fortunately, we encountered none of that type of activity during our visit to Germany. (Such activities began after we arrived back home). But that was probably just a matter of good timing.

To that end, I just read this morning that last night in the German city of Frankfurt—the financial hub of the country—shops were looted by young rioters on the heels of a large demonstration in city center. According to officials, “around 100 clearly identified left-wing extremists” had infiltrated the protest. In their mind, this explains the acts of violence. At the same time, large protests with isolated incidents of violence also took place in Berlin, Cologne and Hamburg. These mostly peaceful demonstrations were organized by a campaign group called *Gen Z regelt* (“Gen Z takes control”) to protest public school underfunding, the high cost of living, public spending cuts and compulsory military service. As some commentators have pointed out, this recent unrest in German cities echoes similar youth-led mass protests in France, where over 300,000 people also demonstrated this past weekend.<Footnote number={3} />

One observation: our world continues to confuse license with freedom. Another observation: these are appeals for humans to apply human band-aids to a deeper social and economic cancer. In my view, the only answer to such issues is Jesus Christ (there is simply no lasting social justice possible without Christ at the center); and—as we have seen—Christ has been exiled from the church.

At any rate, as I recall, these youthful protests then occurring in neighboring France were only addressed one time during our trip. This was by one of our tour guides, a delightful elderly lady who had lived for decades under the Communist regime in East Germany. “If only these young people could have spent some time in the former East Germany,” she said, “they would be more grateful for what they have.”

I thought that was a telling comment.

(If memory serves me correctly, her remark was one of the highlights of a long motor coach ride from our boat to Torgau—a charming Renaissance city built around a dominating castle complex, including Europe’s oldest toy store. Walking down the steps from the castle walls and crossing a busy street, our group of visitors arrived at the beautiful Elbe River. At that spot there is a monument marking the meeting place of Soviet troops, actually a Ukrainian unit, and US forces in April 1945, during the war’s closing days.)

Back to the idea of youth-led gatherings. Only one event during our visit to Prague came close to such an incident. While we were visiting one of the city’s main squares—in truth, I talked the rest of our group into walking from our hotel to the *Hard Rock Café* for a good hamburger and so I could buy a t-shirt—we encountered a large gathering of mostly young people who had assembled suddenly, almost certainly called together by social media messages. The large crowd (building by the minute) was accompanied by the blaring music of a local rock band, numerous Czech flags waving, and a noticeable level of energy that always seems to accompany such spontaneous gatherings. I later learned that they were protesting in favor of an issue on their upcoming election ballot, whether the government should add two free television stations and a radio station for all the people of the country.

That was it!

(Oh, that such issues were the most contentious in our own ideologically fraught elections with all the expensive, repetitive, tawdry, and character assassinating campaign ads.)

At any rate, I still remember on that occasion, as we were leaving the scene to walk back to the hotel, we passed several young people, all seemingly excited, who were rushing headlong to join the throng in the square. (There is something strangely contagious about a mob psychology; history is littered with examples).

Meanwhile, I was content to stand on the crowd’s margin, make a mental note of the energy generated by the assembled masses, and snap a picture or two.

I began to ask myself what is really going on in these types of gatherings here and in other cities? And, as an extension to that thought, why are we likely to see an uptick in such youth-led protest violence in our own country? In my view, it is the conjunction of a spirit of lawlessness—fed by a sense of disrespect for authority—that overlaps with, and is reinforced by, a spirit of hopelessness among today’s youth. Gen Z is the first generational cohort that has no expectation of living a better life than their parents, both materially and spiritually.

And that, by itself, says a lot.

What else makes today’s unrest unique? The underlying fear that an inhuman, out-of-touch, superintelligent, machine-based technology in the hands of greedy billionaires is just over the horizon and could run amok.

No previous generation has had to cope with that possibility.

As if that wasn’t enough, add to that a complete lack of confidence by today’s youth that existing institutions or today’s leaders can come up with answers to the dilemma.

“Sigh.”

Maybe some of you disagree or see my view as little more than a dinosaur’s snort in the foggy darkness.

I hope you’re right.

For the umpteenth time, over the next few weeks, I’ll be sending out missives about some interesting people, places and events we encountered during our trip.

I hope you enjoy them.
