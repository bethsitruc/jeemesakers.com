<p>"The fundamental laws that govern matter and energy cannot predict another fundamental property of life: It is the only system in the universe that **uses** information for its own purposes. Plants grow toward light, microbes swim toward rich food sources, animals hide from predators, humans send giant metal contraptions into outer space... Life is both agential and autonomous."</p>

<p>"One thing that later generations might do with their super-powerful computers is run detailed simulations of their forebears or of people like their forebears. Because their computers would be so powerful, they could run a great many such simulations. Suppose that these simulated people are conscious (as they would be if the simulations were sufficiently fine-grained and if a certain quite widely accepted position in the philosophy of the mind is correct). Then it could be the case that the vast majority of minds like ours do not belong to the original race but rather to people simulated by the advanced descendants of an original race. It is then possible to argue that, if this were the case, we would be rational to think that we are likely among the simulated minds rather than among the original biological ones. Therefore, if we don't think that we are currently living in a computer simulation, we are not entitled to believe that we will have descendants who will run lots of such simulations of their forebears."</p>

<div className="epigraph-author"><p>Nick Bostrom<Footnote number={2} /></p></div>

As another Christmas season approaches, I have been thinking about the meaning of "life" and the extraordinary claim Jesus made about himself (cited above). I have started reading the Gospel of John (in both the English and the German), and, at the same time, pondering where exponentially expanding artificial intelligence (A.I.) is taking us as a species. Like many of you, I have followed reports of the strange comet originating from outside our own Milky Way---the so-called 3I/ATLAS---which will make its closest flyby to Earth (170 million miles away) later this week---and whether the comet could be a spacecraft sent by more advanced life forms elsewhere in the universe.<Footnote number={3} />

Those things, my friends, is a plateful of idea-food to mull over.

Several years ago, I started writing a fiction piece that revolved around a strange scene: I was sitting in a railroad-type passenger car with only one other passenger on board. This passenger, an Albert Einstein-looking individual, was seated near the rear of "Car Eight." I remember being startled when this elderly gentleman, without warning, suddenly stands up and yells, "I've done it! I've figured out how everything in the universe works."

That scene remains so powerfully etched in my imagination that I've never been able to shake it. Unfortunately, I was never able to finish the piece because, in large part, I couldn't come up with a suitable ending. The scene with the eccentric passenger left far more questions than I was able to answer.

What exactly was he talking about by claiming to have uncovered the secret key to the inner workings of the universe?

Was his breakthrough discovery based on revelation-type insight? Or an entirely new mathematical algorithm?

Was the imaginary scene---which remains so vivid to me---the product of my imagination, a dream sequence, or an unexplained vision?

Why was I there?

Or, for that matter, why was he there?

Why inside a train car?

Why Car Eight?

Where was the train going?

Why was the man a grandfatherly figure that looked so much like Einstein? (I have often thought this could have been triggered by my subconscious realization that Einstein was working on a unified theory of physics when he passed and, in his final moments of life, uttered something to the nurse at his bedside who was unable to speak German and did not understand the great scientist's final words. Nor was she able to repeat the words so they made sense to others. Einstein's deathbed utterance remains one of history's most enduring mysteries).

I have learned since that my imaginative railcar scene somewhat resembles the opening scene of *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy,* where a "girl sitting on her own" in the town of Rickmansworth, suddenly discovers the secret to making the world "a good and happy place." But as fate would have it, "before she could get to a phone to tell anyone about it, a terrible stupid catastrophe occurred, and the idea was lost forever."<Footnote number={4} />

At any rate, my scene with the all-knowing passenger, as much as anything else, explains my interest in today's physicists' and mathematicians' efforts to derive a functional theory that could explain all of life---from quantum interactions at the micro-level to Einstein's theories of general relativity at the macro-level. Perhaps I am one of the few that would hope that such an all-encompassing theory of existence would take into account spiritual dynamics.

As I think back on my personal quest to understand the broader implications of life, I can't help but remember Dr. Maurice Boyd. He was one of my favorite history professors during my graduate school years at Texas Christian University in Fort Worth, Texas. He was the History Department Chair, a large teddy bear of a man with bushy eyebrows, a quick, genuine smile and a quiet and modest intelligence. All the history graduate students---me included---both loved and respected him. Those were the heady days of Watergate, campus unrest, fallout shelters, pay phones, *Encyclopedia Britannica,* the Cold War Space Race and gasoline rationing. It was also an age of typewriters, dial-up telephones, phonographic albums, and single-lens reflex cameras.

In other words, a long-forgotten world.

For Boyd's Early Modern European History class, he assigned each of us enrolled in the class the task of reading a large bundle of books.

One of those books changed my life.

Thomas Samuel Kuhn's controversial book, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions,* was published in 1962, and became very influential in academic and popular circles.<Footnote number={5} /> Kuhn (1922-1996) was an American physicist, historian and philosopher of science, whose book made the term "paradigm shift" an English-language idiom. In his book, Kuhn asserted that scientific fields undergo periodic "paradigm shifts" rather than progressing in a linear and continuous way and that these shifts open up new approaches to understanding that scientists would never have considered before. Moreover, the notion of scientific truth, at any given moment, cannot be established by objective criteria but is defined by a consensus of the scientific community.

Competing paradigms are viewed as irreconcilable versions of reality.

It seems to me that Kuhn's ideas are just as relevant today---in my search for the meaning of life---as when I first encountered them over five decades ago. Today, scientific elites have closed ranks in support of politicized science and are quick to drum out any thinker opposing this new party line. Any alternative hypothesis regarding climate change, for example, is ridiculed and its proponents are criticized in the harshest terms (despite mounting evidence that statistics have been "cooked" to support the conventional wisdom). Scientists "in the club" are vying for billion dollar commercial and government contracts to support their pet research projects---they are selling their souls to the highest bidder.

As one of many examples, I wonder what Kuhn would think of how computers (still in their infancy) have changed modern science, particularly the idea that a discovery should be reproducible before being accepted as scientific knowledge. As one recent commentator observes:

> "For most the history of science, researchers have reported their methods in a way that enabled independent reproduction of their results. But, since the introduction of the personal computer---and the point-and-click software programs that have evolved to make it more user-friendly---reproducibility of much research has become questionable, if not impossible. Too much of the research process is now shrouded by the opaque use of computers \[and algorithms\] that many researchers have come to depend on. This makes it almost impossible for an outsider to recreate their results."<Footnote number={6} />

That is doubly true---I respectfully suggest---about scientific explanations of life. Just take one of many ideas, that the universe consists entirely of information (an idea that, at least in its modern iteration, traces back to Edward Fredkin in the 1980s.)<Footnote number={7} />

Just as my brief practice of law made me forever skeptical of medical practitioners, Kuhn's concepts have influenced my skepticism of today's scientists and technocrats.

Today, for example, it is perfectly acceptable to say that the universe is a giant computer and who we perceive as God is really a super-programmer or code writer. Anyone daring to suggest that the universe is the handiwork of a Creative God is completely dismissed.

In fact, my personal belief is that the real driving force behind today's breakthrough scientific discoveries at the macro- and micro-levels---and with it our view of what constitutes life---has less to do with man's ingenuity, or math-based algorithms, than a prophetic promise God made to His people:

> *"This is what the LORD says: 'Only if the heavens above can be measured and the foundations of the earth below be searched out will I reject all the descendants of Israel because of all they have done,' declares the LORD."* 
>
> *Jeremiah 31:37*

Life---a true gift of God---is measured in windows of human lifespans. During my lifetime, for example, I have seen incredible scientific and technological breakthroughs. In the last 25 years alone, we have witnessed: the sequencing of a complete human genome (2003) with an accompanying acceleration of synthetic biology; the first synthetic cell (2010), the first synthetic DNA (2012), the first synthetic chromosomes (2014); the discovery and development of the first gene-editing tool, CRISPR<Footnote number={8} /> beginning in 2012 that has revolutionized medical treatment; the development of AlphaFold---an AI program that predicts the three-dimensional shape of proteins---leading to a Nobel Prize in Chemistry for Alphabet's DeepMind laboratory (2024); the rapid development of RNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020); chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell immunotherapy in 2017; the invention of graphene (2004); the discovery of the Higgs boson (the so-called God particle) confirmed by CERN's Large Hadron Collider in 2012; the detection of gravitational waves; images of a black hole; robotic space explorers (NASA's Voyager 1 in 2012) entering interstellar space; the use of Lidar techniques to find long lost archeological treasures; and, advancements in nuclear fusion power and quantum computers.<Footnote number={9} />

Just in the past couple weeks, for example, scientists have extracted RNA from a remarkably well-preserved 40,000-year-old woolly mammoth.<Footnote number={10} /> (I told my students at Alice Lloyd College over 45 years ago that I was convinced my grandchildren would see a live de-extincted saber tooth tiger---long before Michael Crichton wrote *Jurassic Park* in 1990).

One of the more interesting things that has happened during my lifetime concerns space-related developments. I still remember as a young pre-teenager reading a magazine about *Sputnik* (launched 1957) while waiting for a haircut at Clyde's Barber Shop in my hometown of Springboro, Ohio. I recall watching with the rest of the world as Neil Armstrong walked on the moon (1969). I was watching with my Current Events class at JBS when the Challenger space shuttle exploded (1986). But more recently, I read about the convergence of artificial intelligence and space exploration with start-up *Starcloud's* satellite---equipped with *Nvidia's* advanced GPUs---training *Google's* open-source Gemini model (the first instance of large language training beyond Earth's atmosphere).<Footnote number={11} />

Amazing.

And space-based data centers are soon to follow. (The high energy demands of terrestrial data centers are prompting Big Tech companies---such as *Google, SpaceX,* and Jeff Bezos's *Blue Origin*---to engage in a high-stakes space competition to put AI in space: interestingly enough, Skynet, the space-based artificial superintelligence that decides to destroy humans in the *Terminator* movie franchise was deployed in 2029).<Footnote number={12} />

On this side of the clouds, scientists have announced they have built a sub-millimeter sized robot packed with a computer, motor, and other sensors; a microrobot smaller than a grain of salt.<Footnote number={13} />

Amazing. Simply amazing.

All in my **lifetime**.
