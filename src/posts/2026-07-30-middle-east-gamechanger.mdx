I remember this particular event like it was yesterday.

Do you?

It was - in every respect - a true geostrategic tipping point.

The event happened a little over two years ago - on the night of April 13-14, 2024 (during Operation "True Promise 1")<Footnote number={2} /> - Iran, for the first time, launched a direct, massed strike from its own territory against Israel and American targets in the Middle East. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), along with regional proxy groups in Iraq, Yemen, and Lebanon, launched a synchronized barrage of over 300 standoff weapons, including (in part): 120 ballistic missiles (*Emad, Ghadr,* and *Kheibar Shekan* variants); some 170 loitering munitions (chiefly *Shahed-136* "kamikaze" drones); and some 30 cruise missiles (low-altitude *Paveh* series). At that time, US, Israeli, British and regional partners successfully intercepted almost 99 percent of the incoming missiles and drones before they reached their critical targets, resulting in minor damage.

Good news huh? The campaign, for the most part, exposed significant limitations of Iran's legacy kill-chain. In short, IRGC missile and drone forces lacked the high-resolution, real-time intelligence necessary to pinpoint moving targets, vulnerable sensor nodes, and avoid decoys. Coalition forces, on the other hand, had ample warning time to track incoming salvos and seemingly an adequate number of intercept weapons to blunt the offensive. Electronic countermeasures were effective. When it came to battlefield intelligence, Tehran was forced to rely on low-resolution domestic satellite (*Noor* series) passes and delayed commercial imagery to assess battle damage.

That was then. But what a difference two years can make in today's exponentially-changing technological world.

The success of the coalition forces in mid-April 2024 led to a bit of overconfidence from the U.S. and its regional allies. At the same time, in Iran, the failure of Tehran's assault generated a realization that there was a need for near-real-time, sub-meter targeting and reliable battlefield assessments - as well as a way to circumvent U.S. and Israeli electronic jamming measures - if, and when, Iran needed to retaliate against a future aerial bombing campaign. Simply put, the IRGC needed to accelerate its efforts to acquire or build higher-tier orbital platform and secure communications.

So, move the clock forward to today's U.S.-Iran conflict amid the closure of the Hormuz Strait and a U.S. aerial bombing campaign (As I write this missive on Sunday, July 26, 2026, there is yet another pause in the bombings to allow diplomatic initiatives a chance to succeed). The duration of the conflict and its outcome is uncertain at this point; but one thing is certain and beyond dispute - IRGC forces have been much more successful in hitting U.S. regional military targets than they were two years ago.

What is the difference?

There are several factors, including the fact that - at least for the moment - far-away Israeli domestic and military targets are not included in today's Iranian target list.

But I'd like to focus on another factor which has gone largely unreported in our mainstream press. In late 2024, the IRGC's Aerospace Force secretly acquired, in-orbit, a high-resolution, Chinese-built spy satellite launched in late 2024. The satellite, named **TEE-01B,** was built by the Chinese firm *Earth Eye Company/Beijing Mumei Xingkong Technology*, a private company linked to China's PLA. The IRGC reportedly paid roughly \$36.6 (the transaction was in renminbi), according to leaked Iranian military documents and open-source analysis.<Footnote number={3} /> This satellite allows IRGC decisionmakers to do many things they were incapable of previously. As a result, beginning in mid-March 2026, they started monitoring and tracking high-value targets in the region such as the Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia, Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, locations near the US Fifth Fleet in Manama, Bahrain, as well as military and AI-related targets in Kuwait and the UAE. In subsequent strikes, they were able to hit these targets with increased efficiency.

TEE-01B reportedly captures images at half-meter resolution - comparable to the best available commercial satellites in the West - and a marked improvement over the five-meter resolution of Iran's previous *Noor-3* system. Moreover, now IRGC planners and targeters can benefit from real-time battlefield assessments and can adjust subsequent targeting.

As if that wasn't enough, Iran's IRGC and military hierarchy also is now able to tap into China's commercial space infrastructure to shorten Iran's "detection-to-strike cycle" by utilizing access to near-real-time geospatial intelligence to track new construction and movements instead of relying on outdated mapping coordinates. Chinese firms such as *Earth Eye, Chang Guang Satellite Technology,* and *Meentropy Technology*, have combined to create large commercial observation networks (like the *Jilin-1* constellation), which produces data processed by Chinese ground stations (the *Emposat* network). Many of these companies have been the subject of U.S. sanctions: in May 2026, for example, the U.S. State Department placed sanctions on a Chinese commercial AI startup named *MizarVision* based on social media posts which analyzed U.S. military deployments, particularly in the Middle East, which, in turn, support Iran's military strikes against U.S. forces.<Footnote number={4} />

But Iran's decisionmakers have access to much more than Chinese-made imaging support. Iran's newer generation precision-guided missiles (such as the *Fatah* and *Fath* series), as well as their long-range drones, now integrate data from China's *BeiDou Navigation Satellite System (BDS-3)*, thus systematically neutralizing U.S. electronic warfare and jamming tactics while drastically improving the accuracy of its strike packages.<Footnote number={5} />

As a result, Iranian missiles, drones and cruise missiles in this latest round of fighting have significantly improved their terminal accuracy. As one of many examples, one social media reporter - never friendly to Trump and constantly critical of the recent air campaign - claims that intensive Iranian missile strikes on Jordan's Al Hussein Airbase on July 11 and 12, forced the U.S. to redeploy the Navy's vital MQ-4C Triton drone to Italy.<Footnote number={6} />

Back to my main point: I regard these developments as a true tactical and strategic watershed event. For "middle powers" like Iran, the era when a small handful of superpower intelligence and space agencies held a strict monopoly on real-time orbital reconnaissance is over. As noted by the *Gemini* chatbot response to my query about these changes: "overhead intelligence has shifted from an era of infrequent, high-cost, sovereign exclusivity to one defined by dense commercial proliferation, persistent multi-sensor coverage, and democratized target acquisition."

And what we see with the TEE-01B is only the tip of an oncoming iceberg. A few statistics tell the story. In 2019, there were roughly 2,000 active satellites in Earth orbit. By mid-2026 that number surpassed 18,000 active satellites, driven primarily by commercial and military Low Earth Orbit (LEO) mega-constellations. In dollar terms, the global space intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance (ISR) market has surged to \$28.7 billion, with commercial providers accounting for an increasing vital share of battlefield and strategic imaging. So what? Where battlefield commanders in the Middle East and elsewhere once waited 12 to 24 hours for a satellite flyover to confirm target coordinates, now LEO constellations have cut target acquisition response times by some 38% to 60%\--thereby enabling multi-daily, or in some bands, near continuous revisit rates.

We are seeing results of this dramatic shift on today's battlefields from the Middle East to the Ukraine. And I suggest these changes are having profound consequences now and will only compound in the future. Perhaps most importantly, middle powers, non-state actors, and smaller regional militaries no longer need decades and huge amounts of money to build domestic launch sites or a satellite construction infrastructure: high-resolution optical imagery (sub-0.5 meter), Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) which allows all-weather tracking, and Radio Frequency (RF) geolocation can be bought off-the-shelf or leased via commercial data sharing arrangements.

What am I saying? TEE-01B portends a new world once dominated by our expensive overhead systems. China, for example, has launched over 510 dedicated ISR-capable satellites and has doubled its military and dual-use space fleet in recent years. As we have seen from Chinese commercial entities like *Earth Eye Co.* and *Qianfan,* space data and orbital assets are being increasingly provided to regional "allies" like Iran.

And that, my friends, is a true gamechanger.

At the same time, since such transactions operate under a commercial "cloak," they provide Beijing's leaders with a layer of "plausible deniability." As such, these transactions, technically speaking, are not typical state-to-state military assistance packages. This allows, for example, China's President Xi Jinping to deny - as he did recently in communications with President Trump - that China is assisting Iran with the war.

Reporting on this dynamic has only very recently started appearing in our mainstream press outlets.<Footnote number={7} /> In short, it appears we are sleepwalking and so paralyzed by political infighting, in my view, that we are largely blind to these game-changing, technological developments that are now unfolding in real time.

And more is coming. In addition to the TEE-10B, for example, there is the Chinese-built *Ludi Tance-4 01* radar satellite powered by gallium nitride semiconductors, the world's only geosynchronous orbit synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellite, operating at a high altitude of 22,370 miles (36,000 kilometers).<Footnote number={8} />

Moreover, today's rapid development of cheap but increasingly lethal and ubiquitous automated drone weapons systems which have so dramatically changed the trajectory of the Ukraine-Russia war is playing an increasingly important role in today's Middle East conflicts. As many military experts have noted, drones and new swarm tactics render virtually obsolete yesterday's battlefield reliance on expensive prestige systems like tanks, aircraft carriers, and soldiers in the field. While estimates vary, some intelligence sources estimate Iran possessed some 30,000-50,000 one-way attack drones and loitering munition platforms at the beginning of the most recent round of conflicts (such as the *Shahad-136),* enough to last eight years at the current attrition rate, according to one social media report.

We are also seeing how drones force a cost-balance effect in today's warfare. An attack by Iran's *Shahed-136* drones - which cost approximately \$20,000 per airframe - often requires defending forces to fire \$2 million interceptor missiles in response. And if you listen to the news, we are rapidly running through our supply of such missiles and cannot replace them at the rate necessary.

The bottom line: at the same time Iran's strike capabilities have been significantly improved by accessing Chinese overhead platforms and ground processing systems, we are experiencing a dangerous shortage of weapons to respond to such attacks.<Footnote number={9} />

Let me state the obvious. We are looking at a different world. Perhaps the intersection of artificial intelligence and today's conflict in the Middle East offers a useful illustration of this truth. Even as there is a growing trend of U.S. tech giants to build large cloud-storage data centers in the region, they have become high-value targets for Iranian drones and cruise missiles. As one of many examples, regional AWS sites were damaged to the point that *Amazon* put them in "hard down" status between late March and early April, and the IRGC has directly threatened other American tech companies like *Microsoft, Apple, Google,* and even *OpenAI's* \$30 billion *Stargate* data center in Dubai. Iranian officials assert that such AI data storage facilities enhance American war strategy and thus are legitimate "dual use" targets.<Footnote number={10} /> <Footnote number={11} />

At this point, let me interject one cautionary - and for some optimistic - note here. Certainly, we don't know what dark systems hidden in the "black budget" are being developed by DARPA and in American labs to respond to these emerging techno threats (my rule of thumb is that these secretive weapons systems generally run five years ahead of public acknowledgement). On the surface, and in the public arena at least, it seems that most of the attention instead is focused on the high costs of the ongoing aerial campaign - now estimated at \$37.5 billion, according to U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's testimony before Congress earlier last week - or the rapid attrition and depletion of high-value weapons systems, the rising petroleum costs (oil spiked to over \$100 per barrel after the Houthis hit two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea), and certainly the loss of American lives (18 dead and 430 wounded as of last weekend).<Footnote number={12} />

So, what does the future hold? Or better yet, can we forecast with any degree of reliability the technological "burps" ahead on the horizon? Perhaps not. But at a minimum we are looking at a world of a new generation of anti-satellite weapons, increasingly sophisticated drone platforms equipped with a new generation of AI, the development of counter-drone swarm tactics, a new generation of pulse weapons, and orbital Edge-AI compute systems.

"Sigh."

Minute-by-minute, Armageddon is moving closer.
