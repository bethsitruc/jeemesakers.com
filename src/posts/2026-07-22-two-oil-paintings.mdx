One of the true joys of the human experience is when you stumble across a totally unexpected surprise. That happened to me yesterday.

I joined a group of my fellow high school classmates - as part of our 60th class reunion (yes, it's been that long) - for a visit to the Springboro Area Historical Society Museum in the small town where we were brought up. The museum is located on the main street in our small town and housed in Springboro's oldest building, dating back to 1810. A Welsh immigrant and squatter named Griffy Griffiths initially settled with his family on the site and in the building five years before the town was officially founded by Jonathan Wright. Wright was a Quaker, and they believed that all people were equal in the eyes of Jesus Christ. As such, the town's historical claim to fame is as a major stop on the Underground Railroad with more than 27 documented safe houses (more than any other Ohio community) assisting black slaves from the South who sought to escape to Canada.

Back to the museum building itself. Evidently, Griffy had an eye for the women in those days and his wife kicked him out and paid Wright for the building. The road in front of the Griffith's building became the main street of the settlement (today's state route 741).

At any rate, as we gathered in the main room of the building to register for the Underground Railroad tour, I noticed two oil paintings of other historical town buildings displayed prominently on the wall behind us. The style of the artwork looked familiar to me.

Upon further examination, I was surprised to see my mom's signature.

I had no idea the paintings even existed.

Later that evening, as we all gathered at Heather's - a night spot in the middle of town where you can get a sandwich or salad and a drink as you listen to live music - Lonnie K. asked me what it felt like to discover my mom's artwork at the museum earlier that day. It was hard to pour my feelings into words, especially when the band's loud music almost drowned out our conversation.

"It came as a total surprise," I shouted, "I didn't know those paintings existed.

"I can't imagine how proud you must have felt," he said.

"Indeed," I replied, "my mom was a very good artist."

And she was.

In our house - mom and dad's former house - my favorite painting by mom decorates our living room wall. The oil painting is a winter scene with a steepled, red-brick church peeking out between snow-covered trees. The brooding gray clouds of winter hovering over the church scene gives the artwork a mystical, almost spiritual, feeling.

Mom knew how much I loved that painting and made sure it was left for me. Each of my four younger sisters, in turn, have their own special items of mom's artwork.

Looking at mom's oil paintings yesterday in the museum instantly recreated my emotional DNA link to mom. As I write this missive, I am setting in a chair in our living room; the very same place mom would set, almost always with her well-worn bible in her lap and draped by a blanket. In this place she conceptualized each of her paintings. Many times, as I walk through the living room, I visualize her image sitting here.

I miss her - her contagious laugh, her zest for life, her spiritual guidance, her love which never required an explanation, and her constant prayer coverage. No matter how many close calls I would face in life, (and there were several), or how far I would stray from the path of God's assignment, my mom's prayers would protect me and reel me back into the fold.

A mother's prayer is one of the most powerful tools in God's kingdom arsenal.

My mom lived her life for the world to come and always stood ready to testify about the goodness of her Lord Jesus Christ.

In a way I can't really explain, I see and sense all that when I study her paintings, whether they are in our living room or in the town's historical museum.

Although it is probably poor theology, I believe mom affirms her heavenly existence in ways other than her artwork. As those of you who have visited our modest brick home know, I love plants (just as my dad did). In one of my flower beds is a red tea rose. I bought it the first year Ima and I moved in the house. Last year, the rose sent up a spike-stem almost ten-feet tall - I've never seen another tea rose do that - and the spike was crowned at the top by a beautiful red rose bloom that lasted for weeks (also unusual). Call me crazy if you like, but I think it is a message from mom.

Finally, I try to share mom's creative impulses in my own art. For our class reunion weekend, for example, I designed a picture of a panther with a blue background framed by the words "once a panther, always a panther."<Footnote number={1} /> Barry G. printed the names of our fellow classmates who have passed away on the mat bordering the picture.

We hope it will be a living piece of artwork, with the names added where necessary for our next reunion.

I hope mom would have been proud.
