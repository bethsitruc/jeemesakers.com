Last Wednesday morning---September 3, 2025---an extraordinary event unfolded in this time of extraordinary events which may dictate the future ahead for all of us, especially for us living in what many consider the fading days of the American empire. Three of the leaders of today's most authoritarian regimes---China's Xi Jinping, Russia's Vladmir Putin and North Korea's Kim Jong-un---gathered in Beijing, China (along with a number of foreign dignitaries including India's 74-year-old Prime Minister Narendra Modi), to attend a military parade commemorating the 80th anniversary of the military victory over Japan and the end of the second world war.<Footnote number={1} />

Of course, the most important country in the wartime coalition against Japan was noticeably absent in the celebration, the United States of America.

China puts on these grandiose military parades every five years or so, but this one was conspicuous for its display of modern high-tech autonomous and AI-enhanced weaponry including, in part, new DF-61 ICBMs, truck-mounted high-energy lasers, AJX-200 undersea drones, loyal wingmen, reconnaissance drones, manned J-35 aircraft, and electronic warfare-equipped J-15DT (the latter two will figure prominently as nodes at the center of a cognitive swarm).<Footnote number={2} /> The weapons displayed at the parade served notice to U.S. military planners that Chinese defense industries now have AI weaved into every aspect of modern production lines as Beijing makes significant strides in preparing for future AI-driven hyperwars.

As to be expected, the initial media reports on the event focused on these Chinese modern weapons displayed during the event as well as the surprise attendance of Kim Jong-un's 12-year-old daughter. But in recent days, it has been the "hot mic" conversation between the Chinese and Russian leaders concerning human immortality that has prompted the most speculation. Since this is a favorite theme of mine in previous missives, I'd like to discuss a few ideas concerning this development.

First, let me address the "hot mic" nature of the conversation between the leaders. If you would take the reports of the conversation at face value, then it was a spontaneous discussion between two of the world's most powerful leaders. The problem: in China's tightly controlled society, there are few---if any---spontaneous conversations that see the light of day. In this case, the conversation between the two leaders, as conveyed by their respective translators, was an alleged "hot mic" moment contained in a four-minute livestream broadcast carried by Beijing's state broadcaster CCTV and provided to western media sources, including AP and Reuters. (The broadcast was reportedly viewed 1.9 billion times online and by more than 400 million on TV).<Footnote number={3} /> Two days later, however, Chinese censors did a complete about-face and withdrew the video containing the conversation. At the same time, the state broadcaster revoked permission to use the footage. In a letter to Reuter's, the CCTV claimed the news outlet had exceeded usage terms and criticized its 'editorial treatment" of the material which was, in their terms, a "clear misrepresentation of the facts and statements contained within the licensed feed." Reuters followed suit and withdrew the videos since it "no longer held legal permission" while rejecting CCTV's criticism."<Footnote number={4} />

So, what is going on?

Did Beijing originally approve the feed?

You bet'cha.

Did they have second thoughts after seeing the international reaction?

In my view, absolutely.

What exactly was said? As Xi and Putin walked toward the Tiananmen roster, Putin's translator could be heard saying in Chinese "biotechnology is constantly developing." After an inaudible passage, the same translator says "human organs can be continuously transplanted; the longer you live, the younger you become, and (you can) even achieve immortality." In response, Xi---off camera---can be heard responding in Chinese: "some predict that in this century humans may live to 150 years old." There is no indication that a smiling Kim had the conversation translated for him.<Footnote number={5} />

Xi's remarks not only generated interest abroad, but within China as well. In the aftermath of Xi's "hot mic" conversation, on-line searches for "150 years" was censored on *Weibo,* China's popular social media site and similar posts on other web sites surged over the next two days. The notion of human beings living to 150 years has been a recurring topic in recent years by Chinese media when discussing advances in health care and technology.<Footnote number={6} />

From my perspective, this issue is complicated by the personal (and institutional) incentives for anti-aging programs stemming, no doubt, from the leaders' belief that they are indispensable to the survival of their respective regimes.

My second point is the perceptual follow-on to the story. The Achilles Heel for an authoritarian regime is the issue of succession. When western media outlets began to question whether the conversation was evidence of a thinly disguised attempt to prolong the personal rule of each leader, Beijing felt uncomfortable with the tact the debate was taking.

Both Xi and Putin are now 72 years-old, and both had parents living into their 80s. In addition, both leaders have been embroiled in controversy about their health and leadership capabilities. In Xi's case, after assuming power in 2012, he has pushed through party and government overhauls allowing him---potentially---to serve as leader for life. For that reason, China watchers will pay close attention to the next Communist Party Congress in 2027 to see if Xi will designate a heir apparent. Xi's widespread purges of government officials, including many of his own protégés, makes such a successor designation a risky process. Because Xi's health is such a closely held state secret, any hint of infirmity is closely watched by observers abroad. During a 2019 trip to Europe, for example, commentators noticed he walked with a slight limp. As a result, Xi has drastically cut back his foreign travel in recent years.<Footnote number={7} />

Likewise, health rumors have swirled around Putin for several years based on video footage showing his hands shaking, a twitch in his leg or his odd posture while seated. Despite his record-breaking fifth term in office, unsubstantiated rumors claim Putin suffers from cancer or Parkinson's Disease. Putin's attempts to defuse such rumors by portraying an image of virile masculinity, whether appearing shirtless on horseback, hiking in Siberia, or playing ice hockey. On the other hand, Putin is unquestionably paranoid about his health, reportedly bathing in Siberian red deer's blood and imposing a two-week quarantine on visitors to avoid diseases that may be transmitted.<Footnote number={8} /> Putin remains secretive about succession issues and is an enthusiastic supporter of anti-aging scientific research programs, the efforts of Russians longevity experts such as Mikhail Kovalchuk, and using 3D bioprinting to use lab grown cells to print replacement organs.<Footnote number={9} /> Much of this stems from what Russian experts claim that Putin has an acute fear of death.

Putin's health and anti-aging obsessions also are reflected in his support for government research projects. His eldest daughter, endocrinologist Maria Vorontsova, has received multi-million-dollar grants for the study of cellular aging and longevity. At the same time, Putin has called for a national program to promote medical research into regenerative biomedicine and ensuring active and healthy longevity (according to TASS, some \$2.6 billion will be poured into program through 2030), and 3D bioprinting research.<Footnote number={10} />

For his part, Kim Yong-un, 41 years-old and the youngest of the trio, has several health challenges stemming from his chain-smoking habits, family history of cardiovascular illness, and rumored blood pressure and diabetes issues (he weighs in at over 300 pounds). His health is a closely guarded secret, as evidenced by the extraordinary measures of his aides during the Beijing visit to wipe down his chair so as not to leave any biological clues for foreign intelligence services.<Footnote number={11} />

My final point is that I am seeing a tsunami of recent reporting on the topics of immortality, brain-computer interfaces, and longevity programs. Indeed, much like Xi and Putin, the older I get, the closer I pay attention to news articles portraying the latest advances against the age-old enemies of man: disease, the inevitable deterioration of our human bodies (aging) and death. A recent article in *The Wall Street Journal,* for example, talks about elite investments of over five billion dollars over the last twenty-five years in a quest to live to 150 years and beyond, or at a minimum, extend their lives by another 20 healthy years.<Footnote number={12} />

For those interested in this topic, what a remarkable time to live!

A constellation of more than 200 start-ups and nonprofits involving nearly 1,000 well-healed investors are now chasing breakthrough discoveries in the quest to extend human lifetimes.<Footnote number={13} /> Biogenetic advances and research into the human body's aging process includes researchers now identifying specific proteins---such as the ADAR1p110 isoform RNA editing protein---that protects *telomeres* (repetitive DNA sequences at the end of chromosomes) from degrading.<Footnote number={14} /> Just as in China and Russia, the quest to extend human longevity is being funded by a variety of government institutions as well as billionaire hi-tech titans. Scientific breakthroughs in this arena (including advances in mRNA technology and related biogenetic areas) are almost daily occurrences and promise to have a profound effect on the futures of my grandchildren---and yours. Acclaimed futurist Jane Metcalfe says younger generations are now standing at the threshold of a "neobiological revolution" where the next phase of the digital revolution combined with developments in biology will alter the human race:

> "We are sequencing our genomes, we are mapping our brains, we're harnessing things like DNA and bacteria and viruses and yeast in order to create new species, new opportunities, new energy sources, new drugs ... \[and where\] doctors delete inherited disorders at their genetic root to spare the suffering of future generations while also curing the diseases of aging and extending life."<Footnote number={15} />

Today, unbeknownst to most of us, three important trends are converging that will influence life's longevity for almost all of us. First, an exponential growth in pandemic-related research activities is spilling over into other biogenetic, health-related areas. Secondly, the number of global billionaires grew by 30 percent (according to *Forbes'* 35th annual ranking, 660 such individuals joined the category over the last year---the total swelling to 2,755---with 495 being first-timers).<Footnote number={16} /> Simply put, there are more people with more money than ever before, and willing to spend it on projects designed to extend their own lives. And finally, an explosive growth in the number of *geroscience* health start-ups signal a change in thinking about the process of aging and some of the most intractable diseases facing mankind (the term *geroscience* here focuses on identifying beneficial elements of blood that dissipate as we age and others that accumulate and cause damage).<Footnote number={17} /> One of these firms, *Alkahest,* has identified more than 8,000 proteins in the blood that show potential promise as therapies.<Footnote number={18} />

As a result, *in our lifetime,* well-funded hi-tech titans of the Silicon Valley, Beijing, Shenzehn, Moscow, and Tel Aviv (among others) have joined the human longevity hunt and are racing to cure aging itself. As Piore observes: "Their goal is to hack the process of aging itself and, in the process, delay or stave off the onset of many of the diseases most associated with growing old ... \[they have\] a newfound confidence that 'aging' can be measured, reverse-engineered and controlled."<Footnote number={19} />

The economic benefits to be derived from such a quest---if successful---are beyond my wildest imagination. Even now, billionaire tech titans around the world are placing their hopes in future novel therapies and gadgets aiming to upgrade the human operating system---which they regard as a machine running on bio-algorithms---at the level of cells, DNA and now mRNA.<Footnote number={20} /> Among the recent advances: a method to create human replacement tissues with a 3D printer;<Footnote number={21} /> reducing the time and cost of decoding DNA and mRNA (what formerly took years of research can now be achieved for a fraction of time and cost);<Footnote number={22} /> nanorobots constructed out of DNA strands that can be programmed to deliver drugs to specific targets; and, frighteningly, biohackers who can use readily available data to decode DNA and mRNA (using a massive treasure trove of bio-data from smartphones, *Fitbits,* workout machines and wearable devices). Cloud computing now stores this data and it is being manipulated by artificial intelligence (as I discuss in other writings).

In short, we are in a race to cheat death. Both my parents, who lived to the ages of 92 and 94, defied the odds. As Andrew Steele observes in his essay: "Every year that you're alive, your risk of dying increases by 10%. This starts out innocuously enough: In your 30s, your odds of death in any given year are less than one in 1,000. But compounding 10% per annum starts to add up frighteningly quickly: By age 90, if you're lucky enough to make it that far, your odds of not making your 91st birthday are roughly one in six."<Footnote number={23} /> Worldly statisticians call my parents *lucky*; I call them *blessed*.

At any rate, my parents were hardly alone. The U.S. population, in particular, is aging. By 2035, older people will outnumber people under 18 for the first time in American history, according to the U.S. Census Bureau. Beginning in 2018, for the first time, there are more people on Earth over the age of sixty-five than under the age of five.<Footnote number={24} />

Today, there is a growing cadre of these technological elites pursuing a well-funded, and very real, quest to extend human longevity and, to achieve immortality itself. The motivation for these twin endeavors, it seems to me, is twofold: human hubris (just like Xi and Putin, these elites believe they are uniquely essential to mankind's future and, as such, their lives must be prolonged); and, secondly, they aspire to use human-based technological means to alter God-ordained life constraints. Perhaps the website of the *Coalition for Radical Life Extension*<Footnote number={25} /> says it best: "The deathist paradigm has to go ... It's time to look beyond the past of dying to a future of unlimited living."<Footnote number={26} />

One fact is indisputable: we are all looking at the uncomfortable prospects of a rapidly graying world.

However, one huge obstacle the technocrat-elites are bucking in their quest for longevity and immortality is the conventional wisdom that human beings have a definite expiry date, not to exceed 120 years. In my college classrooms, I frequently referred to this so-called *Hayflick Limit.* The term itself dates back to 1961, when gerontologist Leonard Hayflick, at the University of California, showed that human skin cells grown under laboratory conditions tend to divide approximately 50-70 times before they become *senescent* (no longer able to divide).<Footnote number={27} />

As early as the mid-80s, Elizabeth Blackburn---who won a Nobel Prize in 2009 for her work in genetics---and her colleagues discovered that the "clicking clock" in cells were *telomeres* (as I noted before, repetitive DNA sequences at the end of chromosomes which protects them from degrading). With every cell division, the telomeres get shorter and, in turn, the cells become senescent.

For that reason, much of the recent anti-aging research centers on *telomerase*, the enzyme that adds nucleotides to the telomeres. Telomerase is a reverse transcriptase enzyme that carries its own RNA molecule which is, in turn, used as a template when it elongates telomeres. These enzymes are active in normal stem cells and most cancer cells but are normally absent in somatic cells. In April 2018, the structure of telomerase was discovered through cryo-electron microscopy by UC Berkeley scientists. Telomerase allows each offspring to replace the lost bit of DNA allowing the cell line to divide without ever reaching the *Hayflick limit.*

The bottom line: despite the hope that authoritarian leaders like Xi and Putin to live extended lifetimes, *Hayflick's Limit* has stood the test of time. The longest verified lifespan in recent history was Jeanne Calment of France (1875-1997) who lived to the age of 122 years, 164 days.<Footnote number={28} /> As of today, the oldest living human is 116-year-old Ethel Caterham, living in the U.K. (the oldest living man is João Marinho Neto of Brazil at 112 years). Japan's graying population is home to most of the world's oldest humans: as of February 2018, there were 69,000 people over 100 years old (9,000 men, 60,000 women).<Footnote number={29} />

So, you want to live to 150? (This assumes, of course, that when you reach such an age you'll have the healthy body of a thirty-year old). Or perhaps 1,000 to 20,000 years as recently suggested by a molecular biogerontology professor (pending yet to be created technology).<Footnote number={30} /> On the other hand, you may want to consider the following: according to some recent studies the whole field of longevity research---despite the massive investments of the technocrats---may be plateauing. One study by biostatisticians estimates that even in a best-case scenario, just 15% of women and 5% of men alive today will reach the age of 100, barring some unforeseen breakthrough.<Footnote number={31} /> Moreover, while "aging causes 85% of deaths in the U.S. but receives just 6% of government health research funding---substantially less than research into diseases like cancer and Alzheimer's that aging causes."<Footnote number={32} />
