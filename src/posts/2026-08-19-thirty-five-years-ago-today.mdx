The last thirty-five years have passed in about a second-and-a-half.

Last week, I was reading an article about an event that happened thirty-five years ago, (August 18, 1991).<Footnote number={1} /> It was one of those events that could have changed the course of history; but I have so little memory of it. At the time, Ima and I were in Hazard, Kentucky, living in the Horne house - located a convenient walk from the old gray-stone courthouse and the law offices of Barrett, Haynes, May and Roark, where I was *attempting* to practice law. Ima, pregnant with our second child at the time, was driving to Willard Elementary School, where she taught Special Education, and Kimberly attended a local grade school.

For us, it was a time of constant changes.

At the same time, on the other side of the globe, in the former Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, (we called it the U.S.S.R. then, we call it Putin's Russia now), a group of hardline Soviet Communist Party, KGB, and military officers, calling themselves the "State Committee for the State of Emergency" - attempted to overthrow Soviet President Mikhail Gorbachev. On August 18, 1991, Gorbachev was vacationing with his family at his official summer home (*dacha*) in Foros, Crimea. A delegation arrived at his *dacha* unexpectedly, cut phone lines, and demanded Gorbachev either sign an emergency decree or resign. When he refused their demands, Gorbachev and his family was placed under house arrest. The conspirators then made a public announcement that Gorbachev was too "ill" to run the government and sent tanks into Moscow's streets. The coup quickly collapsed, however, due to poor planning and a fierce public resistance; thousands of civilians took to the streets in protest, Boris Yeltsin (the newly elected president of the Russian Republic) stood on a tank and denounced the coup, and there were massive defections from the military and security forces.

As much as I love current events, especially those taking place in the then-U.S.S.R., my sole memory of those events was a newsclip of Yeltsin standing atop a Soviet tank. Even though I had taught a college class on the history of modern Russia, I was so absorbed in the day-to-day demands of existing that the event passed by me virtually unnoticed.

I think there is an important lesson buried in there somewhere.

Back to the coup itself. Within three days, the coup completely collapsed, and Gorbachev returned to Moscow. But his reputation and authority to rule was irreparably damaged. Within four months the once mighty Soviet empire was dissolved altogether. The fierce Cold War rivalry that existed since World War II - and formed the primary geostrategic global rivalry of my childhood and early adult years - disappeared overnight.

So, why the coup against Gorbachev? Coup plotters were alarmed by Gorbachev's reforms (*glasnost* and *perestroika*), as well as his plan to sign a new Union Treaty that would - in effect - decentralize power to the Soviet republics.<Footnote number={2} />

They saw their empire crumbling around them in real time.

I like to think about what *could* have happened had the coup plotters been successful. In the short term, there would have been martial law in the major cities, curfews, protest bans, arrests of key democratic leaders, a rollback of market reforms, and a media crackdown. Over the longer term, rather than a peaceful dissolution of the empire in December 1991 (as actually happened), in all likelihood, there would have been civil war and violent resistance to centralized Soviet control, relations with the West almost certainly would have worsened, arms control treaties would have stalled, and the Cold War extended. But most historians agree that the break-up of the Soviet empire would only be temporarily paused, delaying the inevitable.

Okay Jeemes, an interesting historical event to be sure. But what broader "lessons learned" are you trying to convey from this event that happened exactly thirty-five years ago?

First and foremost, the fleeting nature of time. For me, the time that has passed since then has been a blur. What about you? Can you remember what you were doing in 1991? Do you recall the August coup?

Secondly, a warning that the greatest of empires can collapse seemingly overnight, including our own. When viewed through the prism of history, we as Americans are about at the end of our time as an empire (and since the final two years of WWI, we have been - by any measure - an empire). The collapse of all empires shares things in common: poor political leadership and a fractured political system; economic dislocation; foreign wars and adventurism; invasions by other peoples who refuse to absorb cultural values; moral collapse; technological challenges; and natural disasters.

We can check each box.

Thirdly, in a time when history is no longer studied, our young upcoming decisionmakers lack any concept whatsoever of lessons to be gleaned from the past. (This is particularly true, I would suggest, of the bankruptcy of far-left political ideologies such as Communism, neo-Marxist ideas of all shapes, or socialism. They have been universally unsuccessful - with the arguable exception of today's mutated version in China. Each of these, by their very nature, devalue the importance of individual accountability, require a "class" oppressor \[usually capitalists, imperialists or any member of a white "ruling class"\] upon which to focus their discontent, rely on the resources of others, advocate violence as the eventual true means of change, and always tend toward antisemitism and anti-Christian values.)

And, finally, we each need to be spiritually equipped for the momentous events that are looming just over the horizon.

These are just a few of my thoughts based on a thirty-five-year runway with the collapse of the U.S.S.R., and the August coup, in the rearview mirror.

It is certainly not intended to be an exhaustive treatment of the topic.

Just an impressionistic brush-over.

I'd be interested in your thoughts on the matter.
