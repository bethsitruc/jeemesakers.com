Ima walked into our living room last night. She had tears in her eyes. I knew what she was going to say before the words tumbled out. "I just heard from Bev," she said, "Laban passed away today."

Although not totally unexpected, the news rocked me to the very core of my being.

I didn't sleep well last night.

The very cornerstone of my faith is that Laban now is in a better place. Shortly after the last death rattle of breath, Laban Sadtler was accompanied by a large company of angels into the presence of God Almighty and is now worshipping the Lord Jesus Christ whom he loved so much. He heard those magic words that all of us hope to hear one day: "Well done my dear and faithful servant. Enter into your rest."

I know that.

Yet I miss him.

Oh, how I will miss him.

My mom used to say that it is good that we as believers endure old age and disease at the end of our days; otherwise, we would fall so much in love with this world rather than looking forward to the next - and we were designed to live forever with the Lord.

"Sigh."

The first time I met Laban Sadtler was under a big white tent on the grounds of the Capital Church, located on Route 7 near Sterling, Virginia - just outside the Washington D.C. beltway. Ima and I were looking for a new church home, largely so that Bethany (our youngest daughter) could fit into an active youth group.

Bev Sadtler and her daughter Rebecca were sitting at one end of a table and invited us - Ima, Bethany and myself - to join them. At the time, Laban was taking his turn at the grill and enveloped in the smoke of cooking hamburgers and hot dogs. That is what first attracted me to Laban: his genuine servant's heart and true humility.

He didn't have to put on airs: that is who he was.

I recently ran across a quote from Moira Rogers that seems to apply to this missive: "The two hardest things to say in life is hello for the first time and goodbye for the last."

Laban made it easy to say hello for the first time. From that first encounter under the tent grew a friendship, and a bond between our families, that would last for decades. We met at Laban and Bev's beautiful home to discuss Christian books (along with Dana and Deidra); would go out to eat together, traveled together, and engaged together in a broad range of church activities.

I simply cannot imagine navigating that difficult season of our lives without the friendship and encouragement of Bev and Laban.

In this process of life's journey, one bumps into several acquaintances, but true friends are hard to find.

Laban was a true friend.

And more than that, a remarkable individual. He had a wonderful sense of humor and an unassuming way of pulling others into his orbit.

What always impressed me about Laban was his steadfast faithfulness in the Lord Jesus Christ. It wasn't a trait that he wore on his shoulder or bragged about; it was a quiet, rock-solid conviction. He was generous to a fault, and freely loved others - regardless of their social status, race, economic circumstances or physical disability. He loved others and others loved him. They couldn't help it.

We shared a love of sports. Laban enjoyed an evening at the ballpark and we both enjoyed watching the Washington Nationals in their new stadium downtown. Laban had struck up a friendship with one of the ballpark employees and we were able to watch a playoff series game from one of the corporate boxes. Laban coached his son and was knowledgeable of the inside mechanics of the game and the background of the team players. It was a joy to watch a ballgame with him.

We also shared a love for a good steak. The four of us would drive all the way to Charlottesville, VA - a three-hour drive one way - for the fantastic prime rib at the Aberdeen Barn. Laban also loved Myrtle Beach, SC, (at one time the Sadtlers owned a home there) and his cousin introduced us to Angelo's Steakhouse. (We now hit Angelo's every time we go to North Myrtle Beach). I never failed to take a snapshot of my steak on a sizzling platter and send it back to Laban - just to rub it in!

Laban could dish out the jokes as well as take them at his expense. One evening, we attended the Outback in nearby Vienna, VA. Because of the size of our party, the waiter tacked on a mandatory tip. Laban took care of the bill and, unknowingly, added a generous tip. The waiter was elated (of course) and treated Laban thereafter like the King of England. As we left, Laban remarked "what a nice guy that waiter was. He really liked me." "By any chance did you leave that guy an extra tip?" I asked, "you know, a huge tip was already included in the bill." Laban was deflated. "No wonder he was so nice." As you can imagine, after that evening, every time we went out to eat I would rib Laban about leaving an extra tip - without failure.

Laban's singing was so poor, that even in church, his inability to hit the right note made him the butt of a constant stream of well-intended jokes.

The four of us traveled together frequently. I'm convinced Laban knew the location of every Starbucks east of the Mississippi River. He and Ima loved their coffee stops (I'm not a coffee drinker). I remember one trip where we followed the Sadtler's to the beach in our own car. Our drive just happened to coincide with one of the worst storms in the history of the Carolinas. We ducked under bridges, kept track of hurricane warnings on the cellphone, and dodged storm cells along the way (sections of the interstate highway were closed due to the weather). For Laban and me it was a great adventure, but it was far less enjoyable for the girls.

Alas, now - as Paul Harvey used to say - for the rest of the story. For the last several years Laban has been very sick. He found it progressively more difficult to move without pain and spent a small fortune on doctors (and outright charlatans) who offered the promise of relief. I never heard him complain about his lot in life. He held out the hope of divine healing until the very end.

The true cause of his physical difficulties - which increased in intensity over time - was never, to my knowledge, adequately or definitively diagnosed.

In those years, after we had moved from the area, we would play on-line pinochle with friends.

Laban loved to play cards.

When I recall Laban's physical challenges, three poignant scenes come to mind immediately. The first involved Laban and his son Josh. We had attended a Washington Nationals baseball game downtown. After the game we had a take a short walk to the parking lot. Laban was having difficulty with the walk. He sort of shuffled along in those years, first with the help of a cane, later with a walker. On this occasion, Laban almost tripped on his shoestrings. Josh stopped his dad, and with the impatient crowd still milling around us, bent down to tie his shoes. I know it doesn't sound like much. But when I remember that scene - and the tender, protective love of a son for his father - it brings tears to my eyes.

The second scene occurred during those same years. Laban had talked forever about going to watch spring training games in Florida, along with Laban's close friend Dan. One year, we planned an outing, scheduled the car-train trip to Florida, and figured out the best time to go. Laban was so excited. A couple weeks before the trip, I received a phone call from Bev. "I know Laban is so excited about the trip, and I know you mean well," she said sadly, "but I'm concerned Laban can't make the trip. There are nights when his body freezes and locks up," she explained, "and he wakes up unable to move. I don't want you in the awkward position of having to deal with that so far away from home. And I really don't want Laban to know that I in any way vetoed a trip he has been looking forward to for so long."

"Sigh."

Of course, Bev was right. Even with our best intentions and fully armed with faith, we were not equipped to help Laban if an emergency situation arose.

As I recall, Dan and I came up with an excuse - a scheduling conflict that made the trip impossible. I apologized to Laban for not being able to make the trip happen.

My final Laban-related story hits me the hardest. As I recall, it was our last meal with Laban and Bev. They had just moved into a retirement community and there was a nice restaurant on the campus. Laban and Bev visited there frequently. They invited us to join them. The meal was fantastic. They introduced us to their favorite waiter.

After the meal, Bev brought around the car and we accompanied Laban in that direction. Laban's body froze up. He could not move.

The waiter came to help.

While the waiter held Laban upright, I grabbed Laban's pant leg and tugged his foot forward. Laban's muscles simply refused to cooperate. One arduous step after another, we moved Laban slowly in the direction of the car.

"Sigh."

I will never forget that.

Now Laban is in the presence of the Lord. Dan told me that Laban was also having problems breathing and eating toward the end. But now, Laban's spiritual body moves painlessly from place to place. Probably at the speed of thought. I know he is happy and waiting for the rest of us.

On this side of the pale, Bev no longer has to cope with the demands of being a 24-7 caregiver.

I'm sure she'd like to do it all again for just one more day.

The same for Laban's daughter Rebecca ("Becca") - a true daddy's girl - and all the grandkids.

As for me, there is a huge hole in my life's trek.

I've lost a close friend.
