It was just a simple question.

Yet crammed full of profound implications.

As most simple questions are.

Several of my fellow high school graduates - the guys - gather at the local Bob Evans Restaurant for breakfast on the third Thursday of every month. In the old days we would talk about sports and occasionally attempt to solve the problems of the universe, these days its more about doctor's appointments and recent hospital visits.

You know, the stuff of getting older.

The pastime of old farts.

At any rate, last week - as we all began heading toward the door to leave - Sammy stopped me (he had been at the other end of the table). "Hey Jeemes," he said, "you're a religious guy - "

"Well, I'm not all that sure about that," I replied.

"What do you think about the government releasing all this information about UFOs?" Sammy asked.

The question sort of came out of the blue.

To be honest, I'm not sure I do the best at responding to spontaneous questions like that. Especially those loaded with implications. In such circumstances, I usually spend the next two- or three-days mulling over what I *should* have answered.

"I personally think that if there is conscious life elsewhere in the universe, they would have to have their own Jesus Christ - "

Barry, standing behind me, said "amen."

"I don't think they've found any convincing evidence of life elsewhere in the universe," I continued, "in fact I wrote a missive about that a few years ago. I'll send it to you."

(I'd be glad to send it to you as well, on demand).

Sammy's question was well-intended. There was no hidden agenda. He wasn't trying to trap me or trick me. He was genuinely interested in my take on it. (I am always flattered when someone appreciates my view on something, whether it be an off-the-cuff question or during my weekly appearances on the *McFiles* podcast).

But when I returned home, I looked up the missive I had mentioned to Sammy. The missive was from September 2020 (during the pandemic) and was titled "Fawn, Parrot and the Wonder of Life." I must confess that I enjoyed rereading it and retracing my intellectual steps in writing the piece. In brief it concerned how I had encountered a family of deer during a walk near our former house in Sterling, Virginia and then I combined that insight with a recent piece I had read about a parrot's consciousness. During the missive I discussed *Fermi's paradox.* Some of you shouldn't be surprised that I made that type of logic leap: I remember a history class at the College of the Ozarks where I began the lecture with Napoleon Bonaparte and ended up talking about flying saucers. (And no, I couldn't retrace the logic steps between the two for a stack of bitcoins as tall as a skyscraper!)<Footnote number={1} />

So, what is *Fermi's paradox*?

I'll quote Ted Chiang from the original missive (Chiang is a techno-science fiction writer who has won several prestigious *Hugo, Nebula* and *Locus* awards and has unique insights into the Silicon Valley mindset). At the time, I had just read a fascinating Chiang article that viewed the universe through the eyes of a parrot.<Footnote number={2} /> At any rate, in this piece, Chiang mentioned the *Fermi Paradox.* He defines the concept this way: "The universe is so vast that intelligent life must surely have arisen many times. The universe is also so old that even one technological species would have had time to expand and fill the galaxy. Yet there is no sign of life anywhere except on earth. Humans call this the *Fermi Paradox*."

In my college history and philosophy classes, I loved to get my students embroiled in a discussion of *Fermi's paradox*. While working at the Los Alamos National Laboratory in 1950, Enrico Fermi - one of the pioneers of nuclear energy - famously asked "Where are they?" He was pointing to a discrepancy that he found puzzling: Given there are so many stars in our galaxy, even a modest probability of extraterrestrial intelligence (ETI) arising around any given star would imply the emergence of many such civilizations within our galaxy. Further, given modest assumptions about their ability of travel, to modify their environs, or to communicate, we should see evidence of their existence.

Yet we do not.

Not so much as a single peep.

But that's not the popular perception. Tomorrow I'm going to the movies with my sisters to watch "The Mandalorian and Grogu," the latest *Star Wars* movie. (Steven Spielberg's new blockbuster hit "Disclosure Day" will hit the movie theaters in a few days and this will considerably ratchet up the alien expectation). These movies, numerous works of science fiction over the years, numerous programs on social media and streaming platforms, as well as the various iterations of *Star Trek* have combined to create the cultural assumption in the West that extraterrestrial life forms are the stuff of reality. Yet, in truth, there is no indication whatsoever that any other species in the universe possesses "metacognition," a quality described by Chiang in his writings as "thinking about one's own thinking, and it's something most humans are capable of but animals are not."<Footnote number={3} /> (Nor, so far, our most sophisticated artificial intelligence platforms).

But what about the public attention - and excitement - that has followed in the wake of the U.S. government's ongoing declassification and release of historical military UAP (Unidentified Aerial Phenomena) files? Thus far, the result has been underwhelming to say the least. And, for the most part, the scientific community maintains that the UAPs lack verified, peer-reviewed data linking them to extraterrestrial life (*Gemini's* assessment, not mine).

As I noted in my 2020 missive, this absence of communication from outer space is not due to a lack of trying by human beings. Billions of dollars are being spent on satellites such as NASA's nine-year *Kepler* exoplanet seeking mission<Footnote number={4} /> and the European Space Agency's *Gaia* spacecraft,<Footnote number={5} /> generating data leading to a recent conclusion that more than 300 million worlds with similar conditions to Earth are scattered throughout the Milky Way galaxy - with the nearest one probably within 20 light-years of the solar system.<Footnote number={6} /> In addition to these probes, there are numerous Earth-based giant eavesdropping discs and sensors, and countless broadcasting devices aimed at the skies.

More recently, since my 2020 missive, astronomers at Cornell University and other institutions have tried to narrow the list of some 6,000 confirmed exoplanets by creating a highly selective catalog of 45 nearby, rocky exoplanets with Earth-like characteristics. Of those 24 are prime targets including: LHS 1140b (a rocky super-earth just 48 light years away, sitting squarely in its star's habitable zone) and the **TRAPPIST-1 System** (a family of seven Earth-sized planets orbiting a red dwarf star, which remain a focus for the James Webb Space Telescope). <Footnote number={7} />

Nonetheless, the silence from the stars is - and has been - deafening.

This discrepancy, the *Fermi paradox,* leads some to call the apparent lifelessness of the universe the *Fermi observation.*<Footnote number={8} /> The logic goes this way: if life in the universe develops along lines of the Darwinian evolutionary hypothesis and modern algorithmic theories, there is an expectation that the universe should be teeming with intelligent life. Mathematical models like the "Drake equation" suggests that even if the probability of intelligent life developing at a given site is small, the sheer multitude of possible sites should nonetheless yield a large number of potentially observable civilizations.<Footnote number={9} />

Yet, there has been nothing.

Interestingly enough, the *Future of Humanity Institute* researchers reached the following conclusion:

> "When we take account of realistic uncertainty, replacing point estimates by probability distributions that reflect current scientific understanding, we find no reason to be highly confident that the galaxy (or observable universe) contains other civilizations, and thus no longer find our observations in conflict with our prior probabilities ... When we update this prior in light of the Fermi observation, **we find a substantial probability that we are alone in our galaxy**, and perhaps even in our observable universe (53%-99.6% and 39%-85% respectively). 'Where are they?' - probably extremely far away, and quite possibly beyond the cosmological horizon and forever unreachable."<Footnote number={10} />

So how do scientists account for this silence? Again, listen to Chiang's parrot speak: "One proposed solution to the *Fermi Paradox* is that intelligent species actively try to conceal their presence, to avoid being targeted by hostile invaders (we call this the "Dark Forest Theory"). Another explanation is that another candidate civilization has already destroyed itself (the so-called "Great Filter" theory).

Indeed, to discover a planet in the cosmos that is Earth-like requires more than a rock at just the right distance from a star. At a minimum, you would need: a "Jupiter shield" (a massive gas giant in the outer solar system to sweep away world-ending asteroids); a "perfect moon" (an unusually large and close satellite to stabilize the planet's axial tilt, regulate tides and facilitate predictable, long-term seasons); and, a "tectonic thermostat" (active plate tectonics to recycle carbon and keep the climate stable for eons of time).

I've also been thinking about the spiritual implications of extraterrestrial life forms. On Earth, and for humans, I've heard it said there are five revelatory sources: creation itself, human conscience (an inner sense of right and wrong), the "law" of Moses or the Bible (a "code" of behavior); Jesus Christ, and the Holy Spirit. Presumably, an exoplanet would lack the final four. As a result, at least to my way of thinking, the religion would be an aberrant belief system, a counterfeit belief system, or a self-actualized system.

Moreover, if life is fundamentally defined as information (organized, self-replicating Code such as DNA, digital data, or a quantum variation), then the *Fermi paradox* can be explained as: we are on the wrong information network, (such as neutrino communication); data compression (signals appear as background noise radiation); and/or, a post-biological transition where consciousness is uploaded into digital or quantum substrates (in short, there would be no need to reveal themselves to other civilizations).

I included this thought in my 2020 missive. Today's scientists - with their hubris - find the creative model for the universe an impossible pill to swallow. They will believe almost anything else. Max Tegmark, one of today's most respected scientists, asserts that "The simplest and most popular cosmological model today predicts that you have a twin in a galaxy about 10 to 10 (28) meters from here." Or listen to Israeli mega historian Yuval Noah Harari (a favorite speaker at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland and various Silicon Valley gatherings): "All the history of the last 150 years can be summed up in three words: 'organisms are algorithms.'"

Indeed, we live in a culture where the specialness of life is vulgarized by scientists and demeaned by vote-seeking politicians.

The idea that the universe, and time itself, were created by God *for* man and as a means to glorify the Creator is no longer taught in our schools or, for the most part, believed. Much less the idea that all of the universes will come to an end one day and be rolled up as a scroll.

"Sigh."

Let me end my philosophical jaunt with a story I included in my missive almost six years ago. The popular notion that extraterrestrials may be hiding among us reminds me of one of my favorite science fiction short stories by Mack Reynolds. The story begins with two aliens meeting in a bar at some unnamed place on Earth. A conversation ensues between the two as they are seated at the bar.
