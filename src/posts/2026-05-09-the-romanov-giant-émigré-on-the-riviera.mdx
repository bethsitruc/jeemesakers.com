This is my second missive dealing with the Russians from the old Tsarist regime on the Riviera, a story I stumbled across during our recent trip to Nice, France and our river cruise up the Rhône River in southern France. As I discovered, the story of the Russian émigré experience in Nice unfolds in two waves: the Russian elites who fled the cold winters of St. Petersburg for the favorable climate of Nice after 1856, (the subject of my first missive on the Russians in Nice), and the sad story of the elites forced to leave their country immediately after the fall of the Tsar, the tumultuous 1917 revolution, and - of course - the Bolshevik takeover (the subject of this missive).

As previously mentioned, the disastrous end of the Romanov Dynasty was accelerated by St. Petersburg's involvement in the First World War - the "War to End All Wars" or the "Great War" from 1914-1918. The pressures of the war led, among other things, to the shooting and stabbing of the last Tsar, Nicholas II and his family by the Bolsheviks in July 1918 in Yekaterinburg. This tragedy was the final curtain call for the Romanov Dynasty that had ruled Russia for over 300 years.

My interest in this historical epoch and the plight of postwar Russian émigrés slipped into my life unexpectedly. I was looking for a suitable topic for my master's thesis in grad school and stumbled across a footnote mentioning a controversial and flamboyant figure involved in the Russian civil war that followed the Bolshevik takeover in the final months of World War I. The man titled himself General Prince Pavel Rafailovich Bermondt-Avalov (1877-1973) and his experience, in many respects, mirrors the collapse of the Tsarist Empire: as a Cossack he was involved in the Russo-Turkish conflicts, served in the Russo-Japanese War, was in St. Petersburg (renamed Petrograd during the war) for the tumultuous revolutionary days, led a joint "White"<Footnote number={2} /> Russian and German *Freikorps* effort in the Baltic during the civil war, emigrated to Germany where he founded a Russian National Socialist Party (on the Nazi model), was imprisoned by Hitler's regime, released per request of Benito Mussolini, later fled to Italy and Belgrade, and ended up spending his final days in New York City. One of the books I have on the drawing board is a "life and times" biography of Bermondt-Avalov with a notional title of *The Cossack Who Would Be Tsar.*<Footnote number={3} />

Stimulated by my research on this figure, I developed a love and empathy for these individuals trapped in time by the collapse of the great monarchies during World War I and the unparalleled tragedy of these displaced personalities. Our trip to Nice rekindled my historical interest in this regard. As some of you know, the stories of these individuals trapped by a changing world beyond their control provides the gist for my historical fiction novels.

Okay Jeemes, but what role did Nice play in all this?

It is hard for us to fully grasp the dislocation and disenfranchising aspects of the Bolshevik seizure of power in Russia in October 1917. The new Bolshevik rulers deliberately and effectively turned the former elite into *Lishentsky* (disenfranchised persons) as part of a deliberate effort to dismantle the entire social and economic fabric of the old regime. The Russian émigrés that settled in places like Nice bore the brunt of these policies. Their lands were seized back home without compensation as private ownership was abolished. An onerous treaty ended Russia's involvement in the war, along the lines of Lenin's promising slogan of "bread, land and peace." The new Bolshevik rulers also: instituted radical labor reforms, abolished distinctive ranks and titles, stripped the Russian Orthodox Church of its land and educational role, nationalized all private banks, established a new secret police (the *Cheka)* to identify and liquidate counter-revolutionaries, and repudiated all foreign debts incurred by the tsarist and provisional governments (thereby wiping out the savings of many middle-class Russians who had invested in war bonds as a patriotic duty).

What was the result? All these measures triggered the one of the largest mass migrations in history (as many as two million Russians fled the country between 1917 and the early 1920s, comprised of those who had everything to lose under the new regime: aristocrats, landowners, wealthy merchants, high-ranking military officers and intellectuals).

During these diaspora years, the Russian community in Nice perfectly encapsulated the transition of the Russian elites from a Romanov playground to a sanctuary of tragic exile. It is estimated, for example, that by the early 1920s, some 15,000 to 20,000 "White" émigrés settled in Nice and the surrounding areas (compared to an estimated 3,000 to 5,000 individuals that constituted the Russian community in Nice in the days before World War I). The biggest "spike" in these numbers occurred in late 1920 following the fall of Crimea to the Bolshevik "Red" armies and the exodus of thousands of individuals associated with General Wrangel's army through Istanbul. Thus, by the mid-1920s approximately one out of every ten people in Nice were Russian.

What was their fate? Many fled Russia with high-value items but no liquid cash. As such, they became targets of predatory antique dealers or pawn shops; fortunes in jewels were exchanged for a few months of rent. Once grandiose villas were partitioned into dozens of tiny studios with the inhabitants preserving the etiquette of the old regime while cooking thin cabbage soup. Former tsarist generals found work as taxi drivers and princesses found work as seamstresses. Moreover, most of them were stateless individuals protected only by "Nansen passports" issued by the League of Nations.<Footnote number={4} />

At the same time, Nice became a repository for the "Old Russian" lifestyle where Russian-language newspapers, schools and tea rooms flourished. The beautiful St. Nicholas Orthodox Cathedral became the center of religious and social life for these émigrés. Indeed, many of these dislocated individuals lived in a bubble of sorts, convinced that the Bolshevik regime would collapse within months, and they would return home at any time. This led to a unique and melancholy culture best characterized by its nostalgia - a deep, painful longing for a Russia that no longer existed. As a result, there were high rates of suicide, literary evenings became a form of collective mourning, and the myth of an imminent return led many to refuse to integrate fully with the French populace (they lived in a state of suspended animation).

"Sigh."

One of the most prominent individuals "trapped" in Nice and the Riviera by the collapse of the Romanov dynasty was Grand Duke Nicholas Nikolaevich Romanov (1856-1929; often called "Nikolasha" by crown  insiders and Nicholas the Younger by others to distinguish himself  from his father by the same name). The Grand Duke's life in exile in  Nice corresponds to the trajectory of the Russian community on the  French Riviera itself: he was once the pillar of St. Petersburg's  global power, then (after the war and the murder of the royal family)  became a leader of a desperate cause to reinstate the monarchy, and  eventually became a tragic - but dignified - stateless refugee.  Moreover, to Russian émigrés in Nice and across Europe, the Grand Duke  was the *de facto* leader of the "White" cause in exile.<Footnote number={5} /> At the  same time, Grand Duke Nicholas represents the pathetic story of the  exiled émigrés. From his *Villa Choisi* in nearby Antibes on the  southern French coast, he represented the paradox of the postwar  Russian elites: trying to command a world that no longer existed and  forced to live on the goodwill handouts of relatives and the French  government.

Just as interesting to me is that the Grand Duke became the target and a key player in one of the most sophisticated intelligence operations in history.

More on that later.

Grand Duke Nicholas was a cousin of the last Tsar Nicholas II and stood nearly 6'6" tall - literally a giant of the old regime - and was named as the commander-in-chief of the Russian Army at the onset of the war. The Grand Duke was extremely popular among the ordinary Russian rank-and-file for his tough but fair reputation and military professionalism. <Footnote number={6} />

The Grand Duke also had a well-earned reputation for personal integrity and the ability to speak truth to power - always a personal trait that seemingly is in short supply. One example of this was his refusal to become Tsar Nicholas II's choice as military strongman to crush the revolutionary activity in 1905. Refusing to accept the office, the Grand Duke reportedly put a pistol to his forehead and threatened to kill himself on the spot if the Tsar didn't accept reforms. As it turned out, the gesture probably bought several years for the monarchy but earned the Grand Duke the hatred and lasting personal animosity of Tsarina Alexandria.

The Tsarina's enduring enmity for the Grand Duke allows me to bring into the picture one of my favorite characters in Russian history, the "mad monk," mystic and faith healer Grigori Yefimovich Rasputin (1869-1916). In my Russian history classes I portrayed Rasputin as a figure who defies any sort of reasonable explanation or pet psychological theory. More recent historical narratives have reduced this remarkable figure to a historical aberration who personified evil, sexual depravity and exercised an almost hypnotic spell over the royal family. This interpretation, however, at least in my view, ignores several facts, among which is Rasputin's extraordinary ability to heal the tsarevich and his unquestioned popularity among the poor masses.

So how does the Grand Duke fit in?

I find it ironic that the Grand Duke and his wife - in a peak of fascination for the mysticism that prevailed in pre-war St. Petersburg - originally introduced the itinerant monk to the royal family and other fashionable sycophantic elite families hovering around the throne. The Grand Duke, however, soon fell out with the strange monk and this estrangement reinforced the Tsarina's suspicions. In a famous scene, as the fortunes of war devolved into a series of lost battles, catastrophic casualties, and overwhelmed logistics, Rasputin sent a telegram to the commander-in-chief at the front, requesting to speak to the troops to boost their morale. The Grand Duke famously replied: "Yes, do come. I'll see to it that you are hanged." Rasputin responded in kind with a prophecy aired to the royal couple; so long as the Grand Duke remained in command, God would not bless Russian battlefield strategies because the Grand Duke had "disrespected" the man of God.

At any rate, the antagonism of the Tsarina Alexandria and Rasputin helped undermine the Tsar's support for the Grand Duke and in August 1915, during the "Great Retreat" of Russian armies, the Tsar replaced the Grand Duke and assumed personal command of the front. It proved to be a disastrous decision. True to his sense of duty, the Grand Duke did not protest, stood at attention, accepted his reassignment and quietly left. The move was a massive blow to army morale, many of whom blamed the military reversals on "treason at the top," German spies and poor advisors. Whereas the Grand Duke, to many, was a true patriot trying to win the war.

As the Tsar struggled on the Eastern Front, the Grand Duke led the Russian forces in the Caucasus to a series of brilliant victories over the Ottoman Empire, including the capture of the Erzerum fortress. He was in the Caucasus when he learned of the February Revolution of 1917 and was subsequently stripped of his command by the provisional government.

The Grand Duke's final escape from Russia, despite several attempts on his life, reads like a movie script: when the Tsar abdicated the Grand Duke resided at his estate in Dulber, Crimea where the local Bolshevik council and local sailor lynch mobs were determined to kill him; he was protected by a local Bolshevik sailor (acting on orders of Lenin to keep him alive until a public trial); when the Germans occupied Crimea in 1918 after the Brest-Litovsk Treaty, he refused a German offer to restore the monarchy; and, in April 1919 - as the Germans retreated and the Bolsheviks returned - he was evacuated by the British warship HMS Marlborough, (along with his aunt the Dowager Empress), and eventually made his way to Paris and then Antibes, near Nice.

But for the Bolsheviks now in control of Russia, the Grand Duke's very existence undermined their legitimacy and posed a potential threat to their leftist experiment. To lure the Grand Duke back to Russia - where he would become the victim of a public show trial and executed - they arranged one of the most elaborate intelligence "sting" operations in history. This effort, called Operation *Trust* (*Operatsiya Trest*), was carried out between 1921 and 1927 by the GPU, (State Political Directorate), the successor to the *Cheka* as the Bolshevik secret police organization. The operation itself targeted key "White" personalities, especially Grand Duke Nicholas, and was arguably the "gold standard" of early counter-intelligence operations.

How did it work?

The day-to-day genius behind the operation was Artur Khristyanovich Artuzov (1891-1937; even though the more well-known Felix Dzerzhinsky oversaw the overall effort). To accomplish their mission, the *Trust* established a shell organization called the *Monarchist Union of Central Russia (MUCR),* populated with former Tsarist officials and officers claiming to be working underground in Moscow to overthrow the Bolsheviks. The *Trust* recruited former monarchists as "bait" (some under duress or as double agents) and even arranged travel for "underground" members to travel to Paris to meet with the Grand Duke's inner circles. To further convince leading émigré circles of the *Trust's* credibility, there were selected "leaks" of intelligence deemed too accurate to be fake. In addition, the *Trust* recruited a Bolshevik administrative official (and former Tsarist officer) Alexander Yasushev, to serve as the convincing face of the fake organization.

*Trust* operatives sought to manipulate the hopeful expectations of the Grand Duke and those around him (perhaps as well as appealing to his vanity). In short, the operatives told the Grand Duke and his advisors exactly what they hoped to hear - that the Russian people were starving and miserable, many in the Red Army were on the verge of defecting, and the country was only waiting for a Romanov champion to cross the border to lead a coup.

From an intelligence perspective, it was a beautiful and for many a convincing scheme. The *Trust* even arranged for a famous monarchist and former *Duma* member, Vasily Shulgin, to "sneak" into Russia in 1925, complete with orchestrated tours and prearranged meetings. When Shulgin returned to Paris he wrote a book, "Three Capitals," praising the vibrant underground resistance centered in Moscow. The book became a best seller in the West and trumpeted the notion that an effective and imminent anti-Bolshevik resistance existed inside Russia. As such, Shulgin's book convinced many in the Russian émigré community that the *Trust* was real. One of those individuals so convinced was former General Alexander Kutepov, the Grand Duke's intelligence chief.

But the Grand Duke himself remained unconvinced. He was extremely cautious and continued to be skeptical of the reports that seemed too good to be true. As a result, the Grand Duke refused to personally cross the border until the "underground" *Trust* could prove that they had control of at least one major city or a significant portion of the Red Army. His refusal to be reckless undoubtedly saved his life.

What is the bottom line? Although the *Trust* failed to ensnare the Grand Duke, it nonetheless proved to be a successful intelligence operation. It neutralized a potential military threat by the White émigré forces in France by making them believe there was an active internal resistance and prevented an invasion by convincing these entities to "wait for a signal" before acting. As such, it successfully managed the behavior of foreign governments and military movements for over half a decade.

What eventually happened to the *Trust*? In 1927, a defector named Eduard Opperput revealed that the entire organization was a creation of the GPU. The secret was out. More interesting, as least from my perspective, is the grim, final ironic twist in store for many of the *Trust* designers and operators. In the late 1930's, during Soviet dictator Joseph Stalin's "Great Purge," most of those individuals involved with Operation *Trust* - including mastermind Artur Artuzov - were arrested and executed. Apparently, the paranoid Stalin believed that if these spies were good enough to fool the rest of the world, they could fool him as well.

There is probably a lesson in that somewhere.

But back to the Grand Duke. When he learned how close he came to being duped by a fake organization, he - along with others - fell victim to a "great fear" that spread among the exiles. Nobody knew who to trust or believe anymore. For his part, the disillusioned Grand Duke spent more of his time on the French Riviera (and less time in Paris) where he lived the rest of his life in quiet, stoic isolation. He attended services at the St. Nicholas Cathedral in Nice and stubbornly refused to engage with the new Bolshevik rulers of Russia, maintaining his dignity as his resources dwindled. His death in 1929, and subsequent funeral was a massive event that drew thousands of sobbing exiles into the streets of Nice. The Grand Duke's burial in the St. Michael the Archangel in nearby Cannes marked the end of an era for the Russian community in Nice and signaled that a "return to Mother Russia" was no longer a political reality but a spiritual hope.

That isn't, however, the end of the story. There is a fascinating postscript to the Grand Duke's legacy. His remains were exhumed from Cannes in 2015 and repatriated to Moscow for a state funeral. His final resting place is the World War I Memorial Cemetery in Moscow, (the *Bratsky military cemetery)* a final recognition of his status as a "soldier's general." The act was a final acknowledgement of the Grand Duke's role as a "typical" face of the exile experience - the man who spent the final months of his life in Nice and on the Riviera, looking toward a home he was never allowed to visit again.
