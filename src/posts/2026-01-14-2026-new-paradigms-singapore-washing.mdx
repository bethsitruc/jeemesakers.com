<Epigraph>
<p>"While China and the U.S. have long engaged in tit-for-tat regulatory exchanges involving their tech companies ... this probe is notable because it appears to be aimed at discouraging so-called 'Singapore washing'---the process of moving a company from China to Singapore to reduce regulatory scrutiny."</p>

<div className="epigraph-author"><p>Robert Scammell<Footnote number={1} /></p></div>
</Epigraph>

2026 has started with a bang! Evidence of these sticks of dynamite can be seen in every headline: the capture of Venezuelan strongman Maduro; regime-threatening demonstrations in Iranian cities; Russia's pounding of Ukraine's energy infrastructure as the war is now over 1400 days old; protests against ICE; unprecedented Chinese military maneuvers near Taiwan's coast; Elon Musk's *Grok* under fire for pornographic images; and friction between two U.S. allies in the Middle East---Saudi Arabia and the UAE---and that, my friends, only scratches the surface ...

Yet almost undetected among all these headline-grabbing events is an important story that has slipped largely unnoticed beneath the radar. This story, I respectfully suggest, in many respects may have more influence on my grandchildren's future than all the aforementioned stories put together. As a backdrop for this story, I submit we are living amid one of history's greatest struggles: the no-holds-barred, winner-take-all, technological competition between our country and China. Over the last five years, this competition has focused on the development of artificial intelligence (AI); in the days ahead, it will likely expand to include quantum computers, biogenetics, and fusion power.

Okay Jeemes, before you venture too far into the techno-sphere, exactly what AI-related story are you talking about?

Like many things these days, this story begins in China. In March 2025, a new autonomous AI agent was launched in the Middle Kingdom named *Manus,* which attracted world attention by claiming it had designed a "general purpose" AI agent that can conduct tasks with limited human supervision. This holy grail-type development was hailed as a critical step forward in AI, and the second "*Deepseek* moment" by others.<Footnote number={2} />

Many of you have probably never heard of *Manus.* As a starter, the project's name is apparently derived from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology \[M.I.T.\] Latin motto "*Mens et Manus,"* meaning mind and hand. *Manus* is the brainchild of "Red" Xiao Hong, a 33-year-old Chinese entrepreneur whose "vision and track record have positioned him as a rising star in the AI industry" (both Chinese and global),<Footnote number={3} /> and "Peak" Ji Yichao.<Footnote number={4} /> Xiao Hong focuses on strategy and product direction while Ji oversees much of the technical architecture. Their earlier collaboration resulted in a company that created a number of productivity tools enhancing existing Chinese platforms such as *WeChat.* That led, in turn, to an AI startup with Chinese roots called *Butterfly Effect,*<Footnote number={5} /> which in turn developed the *Manus* AI tool, a totally self-standing agent, based on large language models (LLM).

Then, suddenly, the startup company relocated to Singapore.<Footnote number={6} />

Why?

There is one universal rule in the world of start-ups, whether it is *Manus* or *Westwin Elements:* the need for large infusions of cash to transport ideas from the vision stage, and off the planning table, into the world of commercial production and profitability. For *Manus,* Singapore offers a "neutral" or global site to avoid U.S.-China trade restrictions. Moreover, *Manus* is not the first company to test these waters: *TikTok (ByteDance)*<Footnote number={7} /> and *Shein*<Footnote number={8} /> also moved their headquarters to Singapore. Certainly, the exodus of *Manus* from China left the impression that Chinese tech startups can only get international attention if they operate outside of China, according to one Singapore-based tech analyst.<Footnote number={9} />

Why are China-based AI startups so starved for cash? The answer lies in the failure of Beijing's leaders to liberalize the country's antiquated financial system, the stunted development of stock markets, and a distrust of private enterprise that scares off investors and venture capitalists. As a result, Chinese AI companies have trouble raising the kinds of funds often available to their American counterparts, and state funding doesn't appear able to make up the difference.<Footnote number={10} />

In this regard, in December 2025, one of the U.S. AI megafirms, *Meta,* (*Facebook's* parent company) announced that it would acquire *Manus,* in an acquisition valued at between \$2-3 billion (including a reported \$500 million retention pool for the startup's employees).<Footnote number={11} /> *Manus* is the first high-profile AI startup with Chinese ties to be acquired by an American tech giant.<Footnote number={12} /> To seal the deal, *Manus* agreed to sever all ties with China. Thus, in the eyes of some observers, this deal represents the first major "decoupling" transaction of the AI era. In China, many within the AI startup community viewed the transaction through an optimistic set of lenses: some viewed it as a "cash out" opportunity for Chinese AI entrepreneurs beyond the traditional IPO playbook, while continuing to push innovation under a large corporate parent.<Footnote number={13} />

What is in it for *Meta*? For starters, access to a new generation of agentic AI and the talent that developed it. *Manus* AI agent helps users write in-depth research reports, plan trips and prepare presentations---more involved and complex tasks than those typically given to today's chatbots.<Footnote number={14} /> Also, for *Meta,* the acquisition represents a decision to break away from simple (and free) chat tools to a monetized tool: *Manus* has a proven subscription model and execution layer---*Meta* is buying revenue, not just research.

Outside the obvious future AI implications of the transaction, why am I interested? For those of you familiar with my writings, you know I have a fondness for Singapore-related stories, especially those with a technological slant. I spent almost two years working in the U.S. Embassy in Singapore and those months are among the fondest of my professional career. I grew to love the city-state's people, modern architecture, efficient transportation system, tropical vegetation, vibrant restaurant scene, geopolitical nexus, and amazing churches. Indeed, I envy two of my closest friends---who provided a family and home-away-from-home for me while I was in Singapore---who are now doing work with a regional missionary organization there.

But there is another characteristic of Singapore that is even more germane to this missive: Singapore is a high-tech wonderland, and its inhabitants are perhaps the most tech-savvy people on earth. During my time at the embassy, I attended several fascinating presentations by global thinkers and cutting-edge technology experts at venues such as the National University of Singapore (NUS) and the Nanyang Technological University (NTU). The city-state is an important crossroad for research: 60% of the population is ethnically Chinese and, in part due to its strategic location and British colonial heritage, the city-state is a rare and true fusion of East and West.

But back to my story. As you can imagine, Chinese officials are less than happy about *Manus* and its brainpower going to *Meta.* The announcement of the transaction was immediately followed by news that China's Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) was launching a review of the deal to determine if it violated China's export control regulations. The review could take up to half a year.<Footnote number={15} /> This adds a dynamic of geopolitical and technological tension that is likely to shape the legal, cultural, and technical landscape for such transactions for years to come. Indeed, the review itself suggests Beijing may no longer respect a change in legal address as determinative: if the intellectual property (IP), the individuals involved, and the core engineering is from China, it will amount to a "once in China, always in China" mindset that will become a regulatory trap for such future startups.

And that, my friends, is a huge issue!

In my view, the *Manus-Meta* deal, the first of its kind, is but the most recent round in the competitive AI boxing match between the world's two technological giants. The heavyweight slugfest still has several rounds to go. In one corner, Beijing's party leaders have little appetite for seeing a reverse brain-drain that works against them. (Of course, the review completely ignores the efforts of Chinese AI companies---now over a decade old---to convince Chinese ethnic researchers and innovators educated in U.S. institutions such as M.I.T. to return home. There was, seemingly, no issue when this worked in their favor.) Perhaps it is worth noting that, according to Jensen Huang, *Nvidia's* CEO, half of the world's AI researchers are either from China or of Chinese heritage.<Footnote number={16} />

Please follow my logic here. For years, the high-stakes tech competition focused on semiconductor chips;<Footnote number={17} /> now it is all about talent and IP software. It is likely that MOFCOM will frame its review as a "technology export" issue: wherein a Chinese entrepreneur takes his expertise, and that of his team, and sells it to a foreign AI behemoth like *Meta* (using an "acqui-hire" approach---buying the talented developmental team rather than just the company assets), all in a bid to circumvent equity-based regulations. *Meta,* for its part, went to extraordinary measures to make the transaction as "clean" as possible: it bought out all Chinese investors (chief of which was *Tencent*) and shut down *Manus* services in China. It remains to be seen if *Meta's* efforts in this regard will set a precedent of sorts for future acquisition efforts of U.S. firms to buy into China's tech sector.

Again, it is all part of today's technology tussle. In many respects, the MOFCOM review process mirrors the activities of the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS), which vets and blocks inbound investment on national security grounds.<Footnote number={18} />

It is difficult to predict how the *Manus-Meta* transaction will be viewed in the future, say 2030. Maybe it will be seen as only a bump in the road of a much larger geopolitical and technological landscape. I think it may have more staying power and certainly provides an important precedent. More to my point: by 2030, the political environment now dominated by Donald Trump and China's Xi Jinping---for good or bad---will be in the rearview mirror. Also, by then, the global competition for AI between the U.S. and China would be closer to a definitive outcome. Recently, *Nvidia's* CEO Jensen Huang described this outcome as shaped by a "five-layer cake" or stack of five factors. First, ***energy,*** where China currently has an advantage of roughly twice the energy capacity of the U.S.;<Footnote number={19} /> access to abundant energy is critical for AI. Second, ***semiconductor chips**,* where the U.S. remains generations ahead in the design and manufacture of the most advanced chips (like *Nvidia's* Blackwell series). Third, ***AI-related infrastructure,*** where China has a clear advantage (while it can take up to three years to clear the regulatory hurdles to build an AI supercomputer data center in the U.S.; in China, massive infrastructure can be built in a weekend). Fourth, ***models,*** the U.S. leads in proprietary "frontier models," but China is way ahead in "open source" models which foster a much faster growing ecosystem of researchers. Finally, ***cultural applications,*** where China has a clear advantage (at least 80% of China's populace believe AI will do more good than harm; in the U.S. much of the sentiment runs in the opposite direction, leading to cynicism and increasing regulatory hurdles). The bottom line, in Huang's terms, China is now only "nanoseconds behind" in the AI race.

The *Manus-Meta* transaction is only one strand---howbeit a significant one---of this ongoing AI tapestry. Depending on the acts of Chinese and U.S. regulatory agencies, it remains to be seen if it will be a true watershed event. Or, putting it in one industry insider's words, will "create a new path for the young AI startups in China."<Footnote number={20} />

I believe it has the potential to be just that ...
