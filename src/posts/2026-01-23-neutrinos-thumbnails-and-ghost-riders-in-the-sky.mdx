What are neutrinos?

Neutrinos - sometimes called "ghost particles" - are among the most abundant particles in the universe (there are a billion times more neutrinos than there are elementary particles that make up stars, planets and people). Neutrinos pass harmlessly through atoms that make up the earth and everything on it, and almost all of them we encounter are produced in large quantities by the sun. Neutrinos are safe - every second of every day, trillions pass through us harmlessly - emit no radiation, have no heat, and do not change in the slightest the materials they pass through. In fact, physicists assert that neutrinos can pass through 100 light-years of lead without hitting anything.

Where do they come from?

Our Sun is a "low-energy" neutrino factory. It produces a constant trillions per second flow of neutrinos as a byproduct of normal hydrogen-to-helium fusion at the core (the proton-proton chain).

Perhaps this example will help: the photon packages of light that warms our faces and provides the sunlight for plants and other life forms here on Earth to grow, take about 100,000 to 200,000 years to bounce away from the Sun's core to the surface because the interior is so dense. While neutrinos are the byproducts of the same nuclear fusion reactions that produce light, they fly out of the Sun's core at almost the speed of light to arrive on Earth in just eight minutes.

My first meaningful encounter with neutrinos came during a presentation by a thinker and entrepreneur who at one time was associated with *Westwin Elements.* We were in Midland-Odessa, Texas, raising money for the startup. The speaker had participated in the design of the detector at the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory (SNO) at a former nickel mine near Sudbury, Ontario.<Footnote number={5} /> The project is one of many around the world that searches for elusive neutrinos. During his presentation, he casually mentioned that at any given time about 65 billion solar neutrinos per second pass through every square centimeter of our bodies (roughly the size of our *thumbnail*).<Footnote number={6} />

My thumbnail!

I can't speak for anyone else in the audience, but that got my attention!

(A couple days ago I asked *Gemini-3* how we know this figure is accurate. The response: we know how much light and heat the Sun produces; to generate that energy through nuclear fusion, the Sun must produce a specific number of neutrinos as a byproduct. Thus, we can mathematically calculate the "flux," the number of neutrinos hitting a specific area on Earth\--roughly 10^11^ neutrinos per square centimeter per second).

Why can't we feel it?

Neutrinos only interact via the Weak Nuclear Force: lacking an electrical charge they don't "bump" into electrons in your thumbnail and hand the way light or solid objects do. To a neutrino, the space between atoms in your hand is like the space between stars in a galaxy - mostly empty. It is not only our thumbnails. An estimated 100 trillion neutrinos pass through our entire bodies every second. Despite this, the probability of a neutrino actually hitting an atom in your body is so low that, on average, only one neutrino will interact with your body in your lifetime and even then, you wouldn't "feel" it.

At any rate, much later after this first encounter with the topic of neutrinos, in 2022, Ima and I took a long drive out to South Dakota. By far the most interesting research being done there now is the search for neutrinos in massive chambers located deep underground. Today's LBNF/DUNE<Footnote number={8} /> facility, which is located about a mile underground in the former Homestake gold mining shaft, brings together over 1,000 scientists from more than 30 countries. As I write, the "big dig" phase is over (began in 2021, it involved the removal of over 800,000 tons of rock from nearly a mile underground to create three huge caverns - each over 700 feet long and seven stories high - to house the neutrino detection modules. These insulated giant steel-lined vats will contain a combined 70,000 tons of liquid argon cooled to minus 303 degrees Fahrenheit).

All this to study neutrinos!<Footnote number={9} />

I'm looking at my thumbnail again.

One of the things so fascinating about today's efforts to unlock the secrets of neutrinos is its potential to exploit cracks and anomalies in the so-called "Standard Model (SM)." One crack in the Model that is starting to appear as we learn more about the behavior of neutrinos pertains to a question that has puzzled scientists for years: why is there is so much matter in the universe.<Footnote number={10} /> According to all human-made models, the Big Bang should have generated equal amounts of matter and anti-matter, which in a perfect symmetry, should have annihilated each other.

There should be just nothingness.

According to Standard Model predictions, neutrinos should have no mass (because they interact via the Weak Nuclear Force).

But they do.

The latest development in this story is taking place over 4700 miles from the old gold mine in Lead, S.D. where Eggenstein-Leopoldshafen, Germany, is the location for one of the key facilities in today's quest to understand neutrinos. The research laboratory is called KATRIN (Karlsruhe TRItium Neutrino), located on the North Campus of Karlsruhe Institute of Technology. KATRIN is the world's most sensitive facility to determine the absolute mass scale of neutrinos. The facility uses the kinematics of tritium beta decay to "weigh" these elusive particles. KATRIN has used measurements based on 259 days of data. Their finding: a neutrino has mass but not much of it; it is at least a million times lighter than the electron.<Footnote number={11} /> Therefore, because neutrinos have mass, they travel at a speed approaching light (in fact 99.99999%*c*), but do not - as far as we know - travel at a greater speed.

Moreover, we know today that neutrinos alternate between three different types or "flavors"<Footnote number={12} /> - a process called oscillation. But could there be a fourth state? That brings us back to KATRIN. The laboratory not only "weighs" the mass of neutrinos, it also is the leading site for our quest for the fourth state of neutrinos or "sterile" neutrinos, hypothesized to be a missing link to dark matter.

Why is this important? If evidence emerges of an interaction between dark matter and neutrinos, then it would shatter the existing Standard Model (Lambda-CDM) model of cosmology by proving that Dark Matter is not a "passive" weight in the universe but rather an active participant in particle physics. The problem: according to recent news releases from KATRIN scientists, no evidence of sterile neutrons has been found.<Footnote number={13} />

Today's search for neutrinos highlights another crack starting to appear in the Standard Model. Why does Nature show a decided tendency to *chirality* or "handedness"?<Footnote number={14} /> This is one of the critical frontiers in modern physics. The SM only includes left-handed massless neutrinos. But, as we have seen above, neutrinos have mass - in its three states - rotate and oscillate, a property that theoretically requires them to exist in right-handed states. Experiments conducted at KATRIN last year confirm that neutrinos are emitted only with left-handed *chirality* in beta decays. Antineutrinos, on the other hand, rotate to the right.

These types of incongruities in the Standard Model may ultimately lead to major revisions, or perhaps a replacement theory. Despite billions of dollars invested in new neutrino-related search projects, we are nowhere close to resolving the inconsistencies.

Why is this such a big deal? In my previous writings, I put it this way: "I am amazed at how rapidly science is changing our view of reality. As a historian, I understand throughout recorded history, breakthrough scientific cosmological discoveries have prompted major perceptual, social, and religious changes."

**_We are due for another_**.

Among my favorite topics in teaching Western Civilization deals with discussions about such transitions, prompted by changes in scientific explanations of reality, invariably lead to great political and social upheavals. For example, the so-called Scientific Revolution - culminating in Newton's breakthrough discoveries of gravity and determinism - was followed by notions of reason and the tumult of the French Revolution. Likewise, the so-called Second Scientific Revolution - culminating in Einstein's theory of special relativity on the macro level, and at the micro-level the quantum theories of Planck and the uncertainty principle of Heisenberg - preceded the seminal political event of the twentieth century, World War I."

Our current cosmological explanation of the universe around us - the Standard Model - is under attack and reeling.
