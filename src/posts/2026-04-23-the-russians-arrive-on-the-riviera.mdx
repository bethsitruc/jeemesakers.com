It seems impossible that it has been a month since Ima and I flew to Nice, France - epicenter of the French Riviera - where we subsequently joined the Viking longship *Heimdal* for a memorable river cruise up the Rhône River in southern France (Provence). It was the trip of a lifetime, and we were joined by our close friends Fred and Rachel Mullinax, Carole Cole and Beth Arms. For me, Nice came as a pleasant surprise: the dazzling view of the harbor and the Mediterranean Sea coastline from the mountain overlook dotted with some of the world's most expensive real estate; the picturesque old city; the charming restaurants; and an enjoyable stay in the *Le Meridian* hotel just off the world famous *Promenade des Anglais* ("Walkway of the English"), an almost 4.5 mile paved pedestrian walkway along a beautiful, palm tree-lined stretch of the Bay of Angels.

All that I sort of expected. What I didn't expect was to encounter the story of Russians in Nice going back to the days of the Romanov Tsars. This revelation began, innocently enough, with an ordinary bus tour of the city. We drove by a beautiful Russian Orthodox cathedral - replete with five onion domes<Footnote number={1} /> - and the guide mentioned in passing that at one time there was a huge Russian presence in the city.

Russians in Nice?

It stirred my historian's curiosity.

And so, the next day, I arranged for a car and driver to take us to the historic Russian part of the city and - with the help of my queries to *Gemini* chatbot - began to piece together the story of the Russian experience in Nice focusing on two waves: the Russian elites that fled the cold winters of St. Petersburg for the favorable climate of Nice after 1856, and the sad story of the elites forced to leave their country immediately after the tumultuous 1917 revolution.

This missive is about the first, voluntary wave of Russian emigres. They were the elite families of the Tsarist world; great royal families that escaped the wintery climate of St. Petersburg for the temperate climates of Nice - at that time a jewel in the House of Savoy - and built huge mansions with interlocking social circles that dotted the hillsides overlooking the beautiful coastline.

Even though I have taught modern Russian History many times over the years, the Russian experience in Nice and the broader French Riviera coastline caught me completely by surprise. As many of you know, my view of history rests on two all-encompassing fault lines: the first is a spiritual watershed, the time when the perfect sacrifice of Jesus Christ took place in the backwaters of the mighty Roman Empire; but when it comes to the second, the physical world and its history, the event that changes everything is the First World War - the "War to End All Wars" or the "Great War" from 1914-1918. Among my reasons for this assessment is the utter collapse of the three great monarchies of the day during the war - the Romanov Dynasty in Russia, the Hohenzollern Dynasty in Germany, and the Hapsburg Dynasty in the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The enormity of the psychological impact of the collapse of these great dynasties is mostly lost on us as Americans, largely because our country has no monarchial experience and because we were the primary political and economic benefactor of Europe's suicidal four years of conflict and the implosion of the dynasties.

At any rate, this is the tumultuous period of history that I love the most. The stories of individuals trapped by change beyond their control provides the gist for my historical fiction novels.

Okay, Jeemes. I get it. What does this have to do with the French city of Nice?

The first Russian "royal" visit to Nice came in 1857 (some sources say as early as 1856) by Empress Alexandra Feodorovna (1798-1860).<Footnote number={2} /> At that time, Nice belonged to the Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont. The Dowager Empress suffered from fragile health and a "weak chest" and following the death of her husband (Tsar Nicholas I) and the stresses of the Crimean War (see below), she traveled to Nice to convalesce. She chose the Villa Diesbach (now the *Villa de l'Officier)* as her winter residence. Her visit established a fashion trend among the Russian nobility. Over the following decades, the Russian presence along the Riviera grew exponentially as the Tsarist elites fled the harsh winters of St. Petersburg and Moscow for the milder climate of the Mediterranean beaches. During this *Belle Époque* period the upper crust of the Romanov dynasty stayed in sprawling estates overlooking the waterfront or occupied entire floors of prestigious hotels like the *Hotel Negresco*; these families included, in part, the Yusupovs (one of the world's richest families including Prince Felix, famous for his role in the Rasputin affair), the Demidovs (wealthy industrialists and landowners), the Apraxins, the Galitzines, the Orlovs, the Narishkins, and the railroad tycoon Baron Paul von Derwies (1826-1881) and his extravagant Neo-Gothic estate, the *Château de Valrose.* (We attempted to visit this "castle" while in Nice but it now houses the administration for the largest university in the city and they wouldn't let us past the gates).

Not only did these families escape to Nice for its mild climate, (the Mediterranean air famously helped "weak chests" and tuberculosis), but also for gambling opportunities at the nearby Monte Carlo Casino and a place to be seen far away from the rigid social protocols of St. Petersburg. Moreover, many of the families proved to be generous to the Nice community and patrons of the arts - following the example of the Dowager Empress herself, who gained a reputation for her support for local charities, funding hospitals and schools. Indeed, her reputation was such that local authorities renamed a street in her honor: the *Boulevard de I'Impératrice* (now called *Boulevard de I'Empereur de Russie).*

But the Empress not only made the long trip to Nice for her health - she was a widow in her late 40's - but at the bidding of her son, Tsar Alexander II, in a bid to strengthen Russia's ties to the court of Piedmont-Sardinia in the aftermath of the disastrous Crimean War. Perhaps just as important, at least for the purposes of this missive, the Dowager Empress was accompanied by her younger son who was the commander of the Russian Navy.

As such, the royal visit was but one gambit in the Great Power rivalry and intrigue of the mid-nineteenth century which followed Russia's humiliating defeat in the Crimean War (1853-1856). As a result of the Treaty of Paris which ended the war, the Black Sea was demilitarized - St. Petersburg was prohibited from operating warships on the Black Sea - thereby denying the Tsarist Empire naval access to the Mediterranean Sea via the Straits. In the West, if the Crimean War is mentioned in history classes at all - that is when history was actually required for college students - it is about the nursing activities of Florence Nightingale or Tennyson's "Charge of the Light Brigade." But the war, which pitted Russia against France, Great Britain, the Ottoman Empire and the Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont, featured several technological firsts: railways, telegraphs, explosive naval shells, logistical and medical innovations, as well as being the first war to feature extensive journalistic coverage and photographs.

For our purposes here, the war exposed several weaknesses in the Russian military, including its navy. The Dowager Empress's youngest son, Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolayevich Romanov (1827-1892)<Footnote number={3} /> - the second son of Tsar Nicholas I and brother of Tsar Alexander II - was a visionary reformer who was determined to transform the Russian fleet from wood and sail to steel and steam. Realizing that if Russia was to remain a Great Power it needed to be a major naval power, (along the lines of the famous "Mahan thesis" based on a book in 1890, which asserts an empire's dominance depends on control of the seas and the ability to project naval force). In this vein, Grand Duke Konstantin spearheaded secret negotiations with Prime Minister Cavour of the Kingdom of Sardinia to secure a strategic location along the Mediterranean coastline under commercial cover as a base for naval activities. The port was Villefranche-sur-Mer (close to Nice) and in 1858, Russia obtained a lease for the Lazaret (a former quarantine station) and the use of parts of the harbor to store coal, food and naval supplies.

Konstantin became a constant visitor to his naval port and the Riviera. Like his mother, he developed a personal affinity for the area and utilized his connections at the Sardinian and French courts to push Russian diplomatic initiatives. During these years, Villafranche-sur-Mer became much more than a mere coal depot; several Russian ships visited the harbor annually, Russian sailors gained experiences in the Mediterranean unavailable at ice-locked Baltic naval bases, the site provided maintenance services for the steam engines now introduced to the fleet, and the fleet changed the small fishing village into a hub for Russian "soft power" that attracted Russian elites and enhanced the permanent Russian cultural presence in nearby Nice.

In addition, Konstantin is one of those pivotal figures in Russian history who could have changed the future trajectory of the country. His liberal instincts were encouraged by his mother who frequently protected her favorite son from the conservative "old guard" in the Russian court. For example, Grand Admiral Konstantin was known as the leader of the "Enlightened Bureaucrats": he not only modernized the Russian Navy, including abolishing corporal punishment, but also was a key architect of the Emancipation Reform of 1861 that freed millions of Russian serfs, and even advocated the sale of Alaska to the United States in 1867 (arguing the Russian fleet couldn't defend it and needed to focus on the Mediterranean and the Pacific).

Most interesting to me, Konstantin also played a key role in encouraging the liberal instincts of his brother's son, the *Tsarevich* (presumptive heir to the throne), Grand Duke Nicholas Alexandrovich (1843-1865). Here is where the nexus between the Russian cultural experience in Nice intersects with one of the most tragic stories in Russian history. Nicholas, affectionately called "Nixa" by those at Court, was generally regarded as the golden hope of the Romanov Dynasty. As a mentor to his nephew, Konstantin encouraged the future Tsar to embrace Western-style progress, promote the ideas of "legal autocracy" (the notion that the Tsar should rule through clear, fixed laws rather than personal whim), and urged the adoption of judicial reforms. Both openly admired the British and Dutch models of government and if Nicholas had ascended to the throne with Konstantin as his chief advisor, there is some evidence that Russia may have introduced a *Zemsky Zabor* (a national parliament) years before the Revolution of 1905 forced it.

So, what happened? In 1864, *Tsarevich* Nicholas embarked on a "grand tour" of Europe (in the tradition of Peter the Great). By some accounts, while in Italy, he was thrown by a horse. The future Tsar became ill. Present-day historians have concluded that Nicholas probably contracted cerebrospinal meningitis. At any rate, Nicholas was brought to the Villa Bermond in Nice where the doctors believed the milder climate would facilitate his recovery. It didn't. The young man's condition steadily worsened. The royal family - including his uncle Grand Admiral Konstantin - came to his bedside in Nice. In a famous Russian deathbed scene, Nicholas took the hands of his fiancée Princess Dagmar of Denmark and placed them in the hands of his younger brother (the future Alexander III), essentially giving her to him with his blessing.

Nicholas's father, Tsar Alexander II, was so overcome with grief that he had the Villa Bermond razed to the ground and a beautiful chapel built on the site as a lasting memorial to his son. (A few days ago, Ima and I viewed the mausoleum and the bust of Grand Duke Nicholas that stands in front of it.)

From a historical perspective, the death of Grand Duke Nicholas marked the last chance for meaningful, peaceful liberal reform in Russia. Nicholas's brother, Tsar Alexander III, assumed the throne after the assassination of his father and became a fierce champion of autocracy, militarism and Orthodoxy. He blamed Konstantin for seeding his brother's mind with "un-Russian" Western ideas and immediately sidelined his uncle, stripping him of his naval commands and his seat on the State Council, thus ending the country's brief and most promising flirtation with liberal ideas. The new Tsar also blamed Konstantin for the toxic revolutionary environment that led to his father's assassination in 1881. As a result, Konstantin's final days were marred by political exile, family scandal (the new Tsar disapproved of Konstantin's affair with his mistress ballerina by whom he had several children), and a grueling three-year physical deterioration following a massive stroke in 1889 that left him paralyzed and unable to speak. Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolayevich died in isolation at his estate (Pavlovsk) on January 25, 1892, at the age of 64.

Those of you familiar with my writings know how much importance I attach to these types of tragic turning points in history and the fascinating "what ifs" that follow in their wake. For example, it is interesting to speculate what the future course of Russian history would have taken if Grand Duke Nicholas would have recovered and assumed the throne with his uncle Konstantin as chief advisor. Instead, Nicholas contracted a rare disease, and his ultra-conservative and anti-intellectual younger brother assumed the throne as Tsar Alexander III. In previous writings, I have discussed a similar turning point for liberal, pro-English reform policies in the German Empire where the "99-Day King," Frederick III died of a terrible throat cancer (reportedly contracted during a visit to Egypt), leaving the throne to an unprepared, erratic, psychologically marred son, Kaiser Wilhelm II. Meanwhile, in the Austrian-Hungarian Empire ruled by the Hapsburgs, the tragic suicide death of the gifted and popular heir to the throne, Crown Prince Rudolf at the Mayerling hunting lodge in 1889, meant the senile Franz Joseph I would guide the empire into World War I.

Historical coincidences?

I wonder.

At any rate, let me put a final bow on the first wave of Russians to arrive in Nice. The last Romanov Tsar of Russia, Nicholas II, used his personal fortune to finance the building of a Russian Orthodox cathedral in Nice. It remains the largest Orthodox cathedral outside Russia. It was built on the same site where the chapel to Grand Duke Nicholas was built; where the great hope for a new Russia died. (Ima and I spent an afternoon visiting the Cathedral and its grounds, enjoying the interior and the elderly worshippers kissing the beautiful icons, and - for a fee of five euros - taking as many pictures as we wanted and visiting the small museum in the basement).

The Cathedral was consecrated in 1912, shortly before the pressures of World War I ignited the revolutionary activity that ended the Romanov Dynasty.

That resulted in a second great wave of Russian emigrants to Nice. A wave of visitors by necessity rather than choice.

But that is the next missive.
