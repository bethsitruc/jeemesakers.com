I often wonder if we are living amidst momentous events---to be recorded by later historians---which catch us unaware even in an age of 24-7 news cycles and instantaneous coverage on social media platforms. My first insight in this respect took place several years ago as a participant in an intelligence exchange in London, England, where we were gathered in the venerable British Admiralty Building. Seated at the biggest table I have ever seen; I was surrounded in the giant room by the busts of past British maritime heroes. At the time, I wondered if the individuals gathered in that same room for post-World War II deliberations realized they were living in the fading days of the once mighty British Empire.

That memory comes back to me occasionally when I read today's newspaper headlines.

One such recent event that triggered my memory in this regard took place a couple weeks ago (on November 18, 2025) in Washington D.C. It was not that the gathering was held in secret or that the media didn't cover the dazzling array of politicians, foreign leaders, tech titans, or prominent sports figures in attendance: that part of the story was covered in detail. What was missing was a careful analysis of a potential fundamental technology shift that is taking place under our very noses.

Indeed, it occurred to me, that the black-tie dinner for one of the world's most controversial leaders would have been impossible to imagine five years ago. Four factors, in my view, made such a gathering possible. First, the ongoing tech rivalry---especially in the race to develop AI superintelligence---between the U.S. and China. Secondly, the desire of President Donald Trump to create a legacy for himself during his remaining months in office. Thirdly, the fact that the U.S. is, for all intents and purposes, ***broke*** and becoming increasingly reliant on foreign capital to fund future technology breakthroughs. For that reason, the Saudi Crown Prince, more popularly known as MBS (Mohammed bin Salman) in international circles, was given the red-carpet treatment in our nation's capital, pictured shaking hands with President Trump, and treated to a formal dinner in his honor, all this after being treated as a "pariah" by the previous administration. Finally, the meeting took place on the heels of a Commerce Department blessing for the export of as many as 70,000 advanced microchips made by firms such as *Nvidia* to state-owned artificial intelligence (A.I.) firms such as Saudi Arabia's *Humain* and *G42* in the United Arab Emirates (UAE).

Some Middle East observers went as far as asserting the U.S. chip sale marked a major turning point in the U.S.-Sino technology competition. <Footnote number={3} />

Time will tell.

That judgement may be, in my view, a bit of a stretch. At best the chip sales can shift the balance of technological power in the region as well as weaning the regional Arab states off their growing reliance on China; at worst, the grandiose visions for the globe's largest data centers will falter at the same time the United States is complicit in building critical digital infrastructure on a major historical and geopolitical fault- line for years to come.<Footnote number={4} />

But one thing is undeniable---in the Middle East, as elsewhere---data has become the new oil.

Much of the groundwork for what happened recently in Washington D.C. was laid by President Trump during a four-day trip to the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar) in May 2025. At the time, Trump announced over \$2 trillion in "historic" investment commitments (including \$600 billion from the Saudis), focusing on AI, semiconductors, data centers and critical minerals. The key deals included export of advanced *Nvidia* and *AMD* chips---overturning Biden-era restrictions---partnerships with U.S. tech giants like *Google, Oracle, Amazon,* and *Qualcomm,* as well as featuring reciprocal investments in American techno-infrastructure.

Trump's trip to the Middle East and the recent agreements in Washington D.C. are the cornerstone pieces of today's "AI diplomacy."

---

Is there anything that has happened in my lifetime that resembles such a potential transformation? I would suggest there is, and it also went largely unnoticed when it happened over fifty years ago (is it possible that that much time has passed?) Many of you reading this missive were too young---or in some cases, not even born yet---when it happened. At the time, I was in graduate school at TCU in Fort Worth, Texas. During those days, I was driving a powder-blue Volkswagen Super Beetle (for which I paid \$4,000 cash, money I had saved during my days overseas in the Vietnam War era). It was a good thing I owned a car cheap on gas. Because of the oil embargo imposed by OPEC countries, (an embargo imposed due to our country's support of Israel during the Yom Kippur War), gas stations were forced to ration the sale of gasoline. How easily we forget. Then, the letters of your automobile license plates determined the day you could fill up.

Richard Nixon was president during those years.

We were living through the 1973-4 oil crisis.

The Arab oil producers had us by the short hairs.

(And were tugging).

Prompted by these events, Nixon's Secretary of State, Henry Kissinger, flew into the Middle East for negotiations. (As a Chinese linguist for the Air Force, I had covered Kissinger's flights into China and Hanoi to broker the peace accords which ended the Vietnam War). At any rate, the result of Kissinger's efforts in the Middle East was an informal agreement brokered with Saudi Prince Fahd that many historians call "Kissinger petrodollars." The arrangement involved a Saudi agreement to sell oil priced and exclusively traded using U.S. dollars and investing its surplus oil revenue in U.S. Treasuries and banks. In return, the U.S. promised to provide military aid and security protection guarantees to the Saudi Kingdom and other OPEC countries, as well as arms sales and agreed to apply diplomatic pressure on Israel to withdraw from occupied territories. The deal---arranged at the height of the Cold War---was instrumental in curbing the oil crisis here at home as well as stabilizing the dollar's global reserve status dominance post-Bretton Woods, fueled U.S. deficit-spending, and locked in American economic leverage for decades.<Footnote number={5} />

Only recently have certain aspects of that agreement started to break down.

But at the time, the dollar became the cornerstone of global *fiat* (paper) currency---indeed, during the Nixon years our country went off the gold standard. Today, there are more dollars in circulation abroad than in our own country. (I used to tell my college students that historically speaking, we are nearing the point in time where the great *fiat* currencies of past empires have collapsed).

Still today, the Saudis remain our biggest arms purchaser and in recent days we have heard of an impending deal for the sale of U.S.-made F-35 fighter aircraft. Both American and Israeli leaders have gone to great lengths to assure their countries that such a sale does not threaten Israel's air superiority and technological dominance in the Middle East.

We'll see ...

I would suggest there were three clear winners in Kissinger's deal in the early 1970s: the U.S. and U.S. oil consumers (especially drivers of gas-guzzling automobiles); the dollar as a global currency; and the Saudi Kingdom, protected by the military might of America. More to the point, in the early 70's, the oil crisis was portrayed as an existential threat to the American way of life; today---I would respectfully suggest---it is the specter of an exponentially growing AI in the hands of Communist Chinese Party cadres that is pictured as the true strategic threat. Thus, both pacts emerged from U.S. crises: in 1973 it was our energy vulnerability, today AI/tech supply chain risks. Both represent a high-stakes diplomacy that blends economics, security, and resource control.

In those days the titans of industry and the petro-complex were the monied elites who controlled our country's agenda and international narrative; today---as evidenced by the attendees at the recent White House gala---it is the AI elites.

Let me tease out this comparison a bit further.

Who are the winners of the recent agreements? First and foremost, large American techno firms like *Nividia* and its CEO Jensen Huang,<Footnote number={6} /> as well as other U.S.-based techno titans such as Elon Musk (ceremoniously welcomed back to the White House after an earlier public tiff with President Trump). The day after the dinner, for example, Musk announced that *xAI,* maker of his chatbot *Grok,* will build a 500-megawatt data center in Saudi Arabia with the kingdom-backed AI venture firm *Humain* using *Nvidia* chips.<Footnote number={7} /> In addition to Huang and Musk, others attending the dinner included *Blackstone's* Steve Schwarzman, *AMD* CEO Lisa Su, Marc Benioff of *Salesforce,* Brian Armstrong of *Coinbase, Apple's* Tim Cook, Vlad Tenev of *Robinhood,* David Ellison (son of mega billionaire Larry Ellison), *General Motors* CEO Mary Barra, *Pfizer* CEO Albert Bourla, *Citigroup* CEO Jane Fraser, and *Chevron* CEO Mike Wirth, and Saudi superstar soccer player Cristiano Ronaldo. Altogether, the group represented more than a combined \$719 billion in wealth.<Footnote number={8} /> <Footnote number={9} />

The second batch of winners are in the Middle East, especially two prominent individuals. MBS, for whom the black-tie dinner was held, is now once again recognized as one of the world's leading and most influential statesmen, following his "pariah" status for his alleged involvement in the murder of journalist Jamal Khashoggi in 2018.<Footnote number={10} /> Another winner is His Highness Sheik Tahnoun bin Zayed al Nahyen, part of the UAE ruling family and national security adviser and intelligence chief since 2016. The sheik is the man controlling much of Abu Dhabi's sovereign wealth fund and is personally close to MBS. A health nut and longevity guru, he is funder of Silicon Valley tech moguls and the guiding force behind *G-42* (see below). Simply put, he is a key player in today's global A.I. wars. He is also controversial because of his long-existing intelligence ties to China.<Footnote number={11} />

Two strange bedfellows indeed.

In addition, two specific AI firms in Saudi Arabia and the UAE merit attention. Saudi AI firm *Humain* was founded in May 2025 under the Kingdom's nearly \$1 trillion sovereign wealth fund (the *Public Investment Fund).* It was launched by Crown Prince MBS shortly before Trump's trip to the region. It seeks to be a global leader in AI innovation and infrastructure. The company has secured partnerships with *Nvidia, AMD,* and *Qualcomm. Humain's* CEO, Tareq Amin, aspires to make the company the focal point of the world's third largest AI market, and in early November 2025, announced a \$3 billion deal with private equity giant *Blackstone* to build data centers in the Kingdom.<Footnote number={12} />

For its part, *G42*---the Abu Dhabi-based AI conglomerate---clinched approval for the export of 35,000 *Nvidia* high-performance chips it needs for the first phase of a massive data center complex being built in the UAE capital. The agreement owed, in part, to the efforts of company CEO Talal Ai Kaissi, who reportedly amassed almost a million flight miles in negotiations with U.S. Commerce Department officials. The chips will help provide the processing power for massive data sets for compute-hungry hyperscalers, AI agent developers, and users in a number of cutting-edge technology areas. (Separately, South Korean firms such as *Samsung* and *SKHynix* have agreed to supply a massive U.S. and UAE-backed "Stargate Data Center Project" in the country as well as assistance in building UAE's nuclear facility to provide a boost to UAE's power grid).<Footnote number={13} />

The third winner---some of you may disagree here---is President Trump and his peculiar preference for personal diplomacy over institutional organs such as the State Department, Commerce Department or C.I.A. For his four-day trip to the region in May 2025, for example, Trump relied on several informal contacts and advisors to orchestrate the itinerary, arrange the negotiations, and engage in deal-making (along the lines of his "art of the deal" style.) Of particular note, were the activities of Middle East Envoy Steve Witkoff, a real estate tycoon with extensive ties with Qatar. Witkoff played a key role in pre-trip diplomacy, shuttle negotiations with regional leaders, and arranging side deals like a hostage release via talks with Hamas. He is also credited with excluding Israel from the itinerary to prioritize Gulf economic ties during the meetings.

Trump also relies heavily on his son-in-law, Jared Kushner, for advice on everything from the sale of chips in the Middle East to informal Russo-Ukrainian negotiations. Kushner---who was involved in Abraham Accords negotiations in Trump's first term---has extensive business connections in the Middle East and with various key players in the region including MBS. Because Kushner's private equity firm, *Affinity Partners*, has substantial Qatari investments, critics have raised ethics concerns about his role in these latest developments.

At the time, Elon Musk also played an important role as a member of the visiting delegation, helping facilitate the itinerary's business sessions (based on his close personal ties with MBS and Sheik Tahnoun bin Zayed), and pitching U.S. AI innovations and semiconductors.

Furthermore, much of Trump's AI diplomacy in the Middle East is being shaped by David Sacks, Trump's AI czar and a venture capitalist who claims that U.S. chip sales to the region "will box China out of the Middle East" and further insists that withholding chips from the region would drive the regional states into the arms of China and "create a *Huawei* Belt and Road" in the Gulf.<Footnote number={14} />

State Department and other White House officials were left, for the most part, to play lesser logistical and support roles.

By now, you may be asking yourself why all this sudden interest in AI development in the Middle East? After all, isn't the region best known for its backward-looking attitudes dating back to the time of the Prophet Mohammed and archaic religious-legal structures such as Shariah Law which inhibit technological modernity? But I would suggest these views ignore a powerful reality: today's Middle East bristles with futuristic-looking skyscrapers, sprawling hotel-resort complexes and banks which now dominate the skylines of the major cities in the Emirates. As one of many examples of this trend, MBS's ambitious "Vision 2030" aims to modernize the Saudi Kingdom's economy by diversifying away from its traditional reliance on oil revenue through mega-projects, privatization, encouraging AI-related technology, and boosting tourism.<Footnote number={15} />

Moreover, the Middle East offers three things essential to AI's exponential growth in our age: money and lots of it, space in which to build ever larger data collection centers, and unlimited access to energy.

Is China sitting on its hands as the U.S. moves to increase its AI foothold in the Middle East? Hardly. Chinese firms provided over two-thirds of all UAE semiconductor imports from 2020 to 2023, and Chinese students and scholars are being welcomed to the country's Mohammed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence in record numbers.<Footnote number={16} /> At the same time, Chinese AI firms continue to make major inroads in the region. *Huawei* plans to invest \$400 million in Saudi Arabia's cloud region by 2028; *Alibaba* has launched cloud regions in both Saudi Arabia and UAE; *Tencent* has recently announced major expansion plans in the region; and *Huawei* is trying to export its *Ascend 910B* processors to Saudi Arabia and UAE. What is at stake? Industry analysts estimate that AI could contribute more than \$135 billion to the Saudi economy by 2030 (and \$96 billion to UAE's).<Footnote number={17} />

The danger of leakage of proprietary AI technology for U.S. techno firms to Chinese rivals remains as high as ever.

The bottom line: Trump's strategy to one-up China in the war for AI supremacy in the Middle East echoes Kissinger's past elite-level shuttle diplomacy strategy, with its priority on backchannel trust over multilateral forums.

Will all these efforts result in a new AI powerhouse in the Middle East that is free of Beijing's ensnaring technological tentacles?

That, my friends, remains to be seen ...
