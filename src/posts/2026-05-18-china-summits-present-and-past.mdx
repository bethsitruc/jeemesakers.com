<p>"Mr. Xi's message to Mr. Trump was clear: 'You're the declining power, we are the rising power ... so the question for you is whether you're going to accept our rise and not resist.'"</p>

<div className="epigraph-author"><p>Nadège Roland<Footnote number={1} /></p></div>

<p>"The summit \[of 2026\] is unlikely to alter the character and course of the U.S.-China relationship long term. It is about managing for stability, not solving outstanding concerns."<Footnote number={2} /></p>

</Epigraph>

As I begin this missive, President Trump is on a plane heading back home from a two-day state visit to China, a summit that was delayed for six weeks because of the military action in Iran. As we've come to expect these days, the interpretation of what was accomplished at the summit varies widely: social media commentary consistently critical of Trump portrayed the meetings as a Beijing "circus" which produced little more than pleasing photo ops and "some performative diplomacy with no substantive accomplishments;"<Footnote number={3} /> In summing up how U.S. mainstream media outlets viewed the latest Trump-Xi summit, one social media commentator observed: "China achieved its primary objectives - equal-footing recognition, Taiwan ambiguity, no structural economic concessions, and a friendly summit aesthetic - while the U.S. achieved pageantry, claimed unverifiable Chinese commitments on Iran and nuclear weapons, and left without the concrete breakthroughs the White House had sought."<Footnote number={4} /> (Perhaps it is worth noting that strategic ambiguity toward Taiwan has been a mainstay of U.S. policy since the opening of China by Richard Nixon in 1972 and China - despite repeated diplomatic attempts over the years - has not been able to get Washington to move off this line.)

Nor did they during this latest summit.

But to acknowledge that would be giving Trump a meager morsel of credit and in today's politically charged environment, no U.S. mainstream media outlet is going to do that.

There are contrary opinions out there, but you have to dig for them. One opinion piece in *THE* *HILL,* for example, points out that "it is China, rather than the U.S. that is on the back foot," largely "because Trump has inadvertently disrupted China's carefully crafted script about American impotence and irrelevance."<Footnote number={5} />

At any rate, U.S. spokesmen framed the summit sessions as achieving an agreement that the Strait of Hormuz must remain open with no toll charges and that Tehran should never have a nuclear weapon - the Chinese readout, in contrast, merely said the two sides discussed the Middle East conflict without further details; the White House readout said the two sides addressed fentanyl precursor flows into the U.S. - unmentioned by the Chinese readout (China has consistently held that it has already done enough on the issue); the White House claimed discussions to increase Chinese purchases of U.S. agricultural products - Chinese state media mentioned only general terms about "mutually beneficial trade" with no specific agreement on agricultural purchases; the White House readout emphasized expanded market access for American businesses - the Chinese maintained there would be a door opening wider on its own terms (on the matter of the large business delegation with Trump, for example, the Chinese characterized it as a courtesy introduction rather than resulting in substantive business discussions); and, on the vital issue of Taiwan, the White House readout omitted any mention of the issue while the Chinese readout centered its entire readout on President Xi's Taiwan "warning." The bottom line is classic diplomatic double speak as each side's readout was tailored to serve their respective domestic political needs while advancing their own negotiating positions.

What was really accomplished? <Footnote number={6} /> The truth probably lies somewhere in between. It will be up to future historians to sort it all out.

I found it interesting that even before President Trump left for his second official visit to the Middle Kingdom, the White House had been careful to downplay the prospects of any major breakthrough coming out of the meetings. As it turns out, this hesitancy was for good reason. Indeed, China sent signals early on that relations between the two superpowers would remain frosty: the China Daily newspaper downplayed the summit, carrying as its headline talks between Xi and the leader of Tajikistan (the Trump meeting was buried in a sidebar), and some Chinese state media outlets ignored the Sino-U.S. summit altogether; prior to Trump's arrival the Chinese Embassy in Washington D.C. published a list of four "red lines" that the visit should not question - the Taiwan issue, democracy and human rights in China, China's political system, and Beijing's development rights; moreover, when Trump's plane arrived in Beijing it was met by China's Vice Premier rather than Xi himself (as compared to the warm personal greeting of Xi for Russian leader Putin in 2024).

Why the largely ceremonial and perfunctory reception? The reason was, at least in part, due to recent sanctions. In the weeks immediately before Trump's trip, the U.S. Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control had announced sanctions regarding twelve Chinese individuals and entities for facilitating Iranian oil sales and shipments to China as well as previously announced sanctions targeting five major Chinese "teapot" industries for purchasing billions in Iranian crude oil (the latter triggered China's first-ever use of its "Blocking Rules" prohibiting Chinese companies from complying with these U.S. sanctions). The U.S., in turn, announced additional designations on Chinese space-related tech and satellite companies accused of aiding Iran's military and intelligence efforts.<Footnote number={7} />

As a result, Chinese leader Xi Jinping's initial public remarks at the first meeting of the respective delegations sounded a tough tone. The basis of future relations between the two countries, Xi insisted, should be based on mutual benefit (read here, China should be treated as an equal and both countries should move toward a multiyear "strategic stability" framework), <Footnote number={8} /> confrontation will lead to mutual damage, and both countries should avoid the "Thucydides's Trap" and build a new paradigm of relations.<Footnote number={9} />

In my view, historians looking at the summit fifty years from now may have a far different take on what was accomplished during Trump's most recent visit to Beijing.

Why do I say that?

I remember another summit attended by an American President, now over fifty years ago, and how the interpretation has changed over time. President Richard Nixon's visit in 1972 has a special meaning for me. At the time, I was a 24-year-old Chinese linguist in the Air Force, stationed at Kadena Air Force Base in Okinawa, Japan and flying support missions off the coast of Vietnam in the back end of an RC-135 "hog nose" intelligence platform. As I recall, we were informed of possible Chinese reaction to aircraft carrying first Henry Kissinger, then the ping-pong team, and finally President Richard Nixon's Air Force One as they flew into China. It was almost a sideshow for us; in those days our primary job was participating in the air war over Vietnam. Indeed, it wasn't until much later that we grew to fully appreciate how important those events were, and why the visit was famously dubbed "the week that changed the world."

As a result of these special memories, I thought it may be worthwhile to compare and contrast these two presidential visits, especially viewing both events through a technological set of lenses.

I hope you agree.

The first and most obvious comparison is the world situation and geostrategic context in which the respective state visits took place. In 1972, the world was defined by a bipolar Cold war between our country and the Soviet Union where we were bogged down in the Vietnam War and looking for an exit. At the same time, China was diplomatically isolated from the rest of the world, domestically recovering from the disastrous chaos of the Cultural Revolution as well as facing a genuine military threat from the Soviet Union along their shared border. Today, the tension between the two leading global powers - the U.S. and China - is as much about supply chains, market access and a technological rivalry as it is diplomacy (reflected in the presence of as many as 16-17 major business CEOs such as Elon Musk, Jensen Huang, Tim Cook, and the heads of Boeing, Goldman Sachs, and BlackRock in the visiting delegation).

I'll leave it to the talking heads to figure out who won or lost the summit and the impact - if any - on the crisis in the Straits of Hormuz and the upcoming midterm elections.

I'm far more interested in how the summit discussions were influenced (and touched upon) today's technological environment in comparison to the situation over fifty years ago. As many of you know, this is the issue nearest and dearest to my heart. I rather suspect, that during the more than two hours of private talks between Trump and Xi - over tea and lunch at the secretive Chinese leadership compound at Zhongnanhai, next to the Forbidden City - on the final day of the summit,<Footnote number={10} /> a whole range of technological issues came to the fore. Especially, I would respectfully suggest, the need to set mutually acceptable guardrails for the exponentially growing area of artificial intelligence. The discussion probably wasn't limited to chatbots, but rather about a future of autonomous killing machines and cyber-infrastructure. I would hope both leaders discussed the "black swan" of a potential AI arms race accident and the need to establish "dead man switches," that is mutual protocols to ensure humans remain in the loop of lethal decision-making. Perhaps even "quantum neutrality" - an agreement not to use emerging quantum capabilities to destabilize the global financial system.

Of course, we have no way of knowing for sure.

Maybe that is just wishful thinking on my part.

In my view, these classified conversations on the last day of the summit are just as secretive as those that took place over fifty years ago. At that time, the most sensitive and secret discussions concerned what was then the technological edge: nuclear developments. China had only recently become a member of the nuclear club. In mid-October 1964, Beijing had successfully conducted its first nuclear test, codenamed "Project 596," at the Lop Nur test site in Xinjiang.<Footnote number={11} /> For Chinese strongman and revolutionary icon Mao Zedong (1893-1976), who was frail in health at the time, there was a genuine fear of Soviet invasion. Following bloody Sino-Soviet border clashes in 1969, Moscow amassed nearly 50 divisions along the border and Mao feared a preemptive nuclear strike. But even though Mao had a nuclear weapon as a deterrent, China lacked satellite intelligence.

Indeed, the true breakthrough at the summit came about because of one of the most unique relationships in history. U.S. policy was scripted during those years by Henry Kissinger (1923-2023), then national security advisor, and his Chinese counterpart, Zhou Enlai (1898-1976), a Chinese statesman who helped shape China's foreign policy in his capacity as the first Premier of the People's Republic of China. Beginning with exchanges during his secret July 1971 trip to China and continuing through the historic February 1972 summit, Henry Kissinger provided Zhou Enlai with top-secret satellite intelligence on Soviet military positions, armor movements, and tactical air force deployments arrayed against China along the border, all based on U.S. eyes-in-the-sky overhead surveillance capabilities. In addition, Kissinger briefed Zhou Enlai on Moscow's strategic intentions (based a secret proposal from Moscow for a joint exchange of information to monitor and potentially neutralize China's burgeoning nuclear program). Kissinger explained that the U.S. intended to manage its own détente with Moscow while using the "China card" to keep the Soviets off-balance. These briefings were kept secret from the State Department. Moreover, these intelligence briefings were remarkable because at the time the U.S. and the People's Republic of China had no formal diplomatic relations, were ideological adversaries, and had fought each other just two decades previously in Korea.<Footnote number={12} />

One of the tragedies of today's Sino-U.S. relationship, in my view, is the absence of two figures such as Kissinger and Zhou who can rise above domestic political equities to act in the best interests of their respective countries.

You may disagree.

At any rate, if nuclear technology dominated the events of 1972, what is the case today? More to the point, did the Trump-Xi summit attain any sort of meaningful progress relating to controlling the growing technological competition in artificial intelligence which some experts compare to a Cold War-style nuclear arms race. This is, in my view, the techno-geostrategic issue of the day.

What is the urgency?

Concerns have grown with *Anthropic's* recent release of the powerful *Mythos Claude* model, especially after China was excluded from early access to preview the model.<Footnote number={13} /> Just weeks before the summit, *Anthropic* published a technical assessment warning that its new model possessed unprecedented cybersecurity capabilities, including the ability to expose tens of thousands of unpatched vulnerabilities in critical global infrastructure. This disclosure, combined with data showing an 89% surge in AI-enabled cyberattacks over the previous year, created an urgent and shared dilemma for both Washington and Beijing. In short, the terrifying reality that frontier AI could be weaponized by non-state actors, terrorists or criminal networks to target critical infrastructure or develop bioweapons.<Footnote number={14} />

In addition to the *Mythos* issue, the U.S. side, in particular, was eager to address the problem of industrial-scale "AI distillation" attacks. In the weeks leading up to the summit, three top U.S. AI labs disclosed they had detected massive, coordinated campaigns (frequently routed through proxy networks) to illicitly "distill" (extract and replicate) the capabilities of proprietary American frontier models.<Footnote number={15} /> These revelations were compounded by reports which surfaced alleging that China-based tech firm *DeepSeek's* newest V4 model may have bypassed Western chokepoints by using illicitly obtained distilled U.S. firm data and advanced *Nvidia* chips, allowing the firm to leapfrog billions of dollars in R&D costs despite U.S. export controls.<Footnote number={16} />

What happened at the summit? If there was a discussion of "guardrails" or these other issues, it was not reflected in the official post-summit readouts. Neither side explicitly highlighted any major breakthrough or extensive discussions regarding this area.

Why?

Despite summit lead-up talks suggesting both nations wanted to establish guardrails to prevent an AI arms race, a stalemate currently exists between the two AI superpowers. The U.S. continues to focus on protecting its competitive lead through semiconductor export controls and cracking down on technology "distillation" (copying), while, at the same time, China is actively moving to protect its domestic tech market and break through Western chokepoints. Since there was no consensus reached at the summit on AI governance or technology sharing, both sides chose to leave it out of their official post-summit summaries.<Footnote number={17} />

Nor do I expect any breakthroughs regarding AI at the three remaining summits planned for the rest of the year. A reciprocal state visit by President Xi to the U.S. will take place in September, talks at an Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meeting in China will be held in November, and a Group of 20 gathering in Florida in December.

In another fifty years, I respectfully suggest, (if the world lasts that long) historians will make a judgement call as to whether these summits will be remembered for orchestrating the first "Great Tech Truce." To be sure, 1972 was a gamble to open the door on a new relationship, whereas 2026 is more of a calculation on how to survive an ongoing techno rivalry.

I have my doubts that much will change.

Where are the likes of Kissinger and Zhou when you need them?
